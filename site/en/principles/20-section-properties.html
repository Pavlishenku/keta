Chapter 20 Section properties

The beam element (chapter 3) sees only a few numbers of the cross-section: the area A, the second moments of area Iy​ and Iz​, the torsion constant J, the shear areas Avy​ and Avz​, and, for non-uniform torsion (chapter 19), the warping constant Iw​ referred to the shear centre. This chapter explains how keta obtains these numbers and how it goes back from the bar to the section to give the stresses at a point. Three levels of computation coexist: closed forms for parametric sections and rolled sections, which are instantaneous; a thin-walled theory, without a mesh, for open sections and multi-cell boxes; and the finite element solution of the Saint-Venant problem on an arbitrary polygonal section, which serves as the reference for the other two. The chapter also covers the share of the shear force carried by the inclined chords of a beam of variable depth. Reinforced and prestressed concrete sections, whose behaviour is not linear, are the subject of chapter 21.

20.1 Assumptions#

  • the section is plane, in the local (y,z) axes of the bar; x is the axis of the bar. The properties are computed at the elastic centroid;
  • the material is linearly elastic and isotropic; a section made of several materials is transformed by the ratio of the Young's moduli;
  • the theory is that of Saint-Venant: the section deforms freely out of its plane (warping) and keeps its shape in its plane; it does not distort;
  • the normal stresses follow the Navier-Bernoulli assumption; the shear stresses are those of Saint-Venant uniform torsion and simple bending;
  • curved edges are represented by polygons; the section is in one piece (several disjoint regions are refused by the warping computation);
  • in thin-walled theory, each wall is a straight strip of constant or linearly varying thickness, the thickness being small compared with the length; shear lag is not represented.

20.2 Section library#

20.2.1 Parametric shapes#

Six shapes are described by their dimensions: the rectangle, the solid circle, the circular tube, the doubly symmetric I section (with or without root fillets), the rectangular box of uniform thickness per wall and the tee with a top flange. Their properties are closed forms. A seventh, generic shape receives its properties as they are, without geometry; it allows neither the computation of stresses at a point nor that of the shear areas.

For the rectangle of sides a≥c, the torsion constant is the Saint-Venant series (Timoshenko and Goodier, Theory of Elasticity, chap. 10), summed until the current term becomes negligible:

20.2.2 Rolled sections#

The catalogue contains the European sections IPE 80 to 600 and HEA, HEB, HEM 100 to 1000, with the nominal dimensions of EN 10365: h, b, tw​, tf​ and the root radius r. The four fillets are circular spandrels, an r×r square minus a quarter disc, counted exactly in A, Iy​, Iz​, Wel​ and Wpl​. A spandrel has as area, distance from its centroid to the two faces of the corner, and second moment of area about its own axis

As​=(1−4π​)r2,d=1−π/45/6−π/4​r,Is​=(1−165π​)r4−As​d2(20.3)

and it is placed at the lever arm h/2−tf​−d for major-axis bending, tw​/2+d for the minor axis. The torsion constant with fillets follows El Darwish and Johnston (1965), the rule from which the European tables derive:

J=32​btf3​+3(h−2tf​)tw3​​+2αD4−0.420tf4​,D=2r+tf​(tf​+r)2+tw​(r+tw​/4)​(20.4)

where α is a polynomial in tw​/tf​ and r/tf​. The fallback web shear area is that of EN 1993-1-1 § 6.2.6(3)a, Avz​=A−2btf​+(tw​+2r)tf​≥hw​tw​. For an IPE 300, keta obtains A=53.81 cm2, Iy​=8356 cm4, Iz​=603.8 cm4 and Iw​=125.9⋅103 cm6, the tabulated values; J=19.92 cm4, within 1 % of the table (20.12). Without the fillets, the area would be 3 to 4 % too low and J 10 to 30 % too low.

Equal-leg angles (EN 10056-1), INP beams with tapered flanges (DIN 1025-1) and UPE parallel-flange channels (DIN 1026-2) are described by their outline, root and toe fillets included, drawn as arcs tangent to the adjacent faces and divided into 256 chords per fillet; the area deficit of an arc so divided is r2(θ−sinθ)/2 per chord, about 5⋅10−6r2 per fillet, that is less than 10−6 of the area of a usual section. Their properties are those of a polygon, product of inertia and principal axes included.

20.2.3 Polygons and imported outlines#

Any other section is described as an outer polygon pierced by voids: bridge boxes and tees, bulb beams, built-up sections. The orientation of the outlines is normalised by the computation. An outline may be read from a DXF file in text format (LWPOLYLINE, POLYLINE, LINE and ARC entities): isolated segments and arcs are chained into closed loops, the largest loop becomes the outline, the others voids. Arcs and polyline bulges are divided into chords under a sagitta tolerance; the angular step Δφ satisfies

R(1−cos2Δφ​)≤εf​,εf​=10−3R by default(20.5)

that is, about 71 chords per turn, and an area error of the order of 1.3⋅10−3 of each sector, smaller than the error of the subsequent mesh. Splines, blocks and the binary format are refused rather than silently straightened.

20.3 Geometric properties#

For a polygon, the area, the first moments and the second moments of area are surface integrals that Green's theorem reduces to a sum over the sides. For a side going from (y0​,z0​) to (y1​,z1​), with c=y0​z1​−y1​z0​:

A∫z2dA​=∑2c​,=∑12c(z02​+z0​z1​+z12​)​,​Sy​=∫zdA∫yzdA​=∑6c(z0​+z1​)​,=∑24c(y0​z1​+2y0​z0​+2y1​z1​+y1​z0​)​​(20.6)

the voids being traversed in the opposite direction to the outer outline. These sums are exact for the polygon. The moments are then referred to the centroid, Iy​=∫z2dA−AzG2​, and the principal axes follow:

I1,2​=2Iy​+Iz​​±(2Iy​−Iz​​)2+Iyz2​​(20.7)

The beam element assumes that y and z are principal. keta does not rotate the section by itself: a polygon whose product of inertia exceeds 5 % of the larger second moment of area is refused, with a message asking for it to be entered in its principal axes. The computation of the properties alone (without a bar), on the other hand, gives Iyz​, I1​, I2​ and the principal angle for any section: on a 100 × 10 mm angle with sharp corners, Iy​=180.0 cm4, Iyz​=−106.6 cm4 and I1​=286.6 cm4, the values of the decomposition into two rectangles.

The elastic section modulus of a polygon is computed on the vertices of the outer outline furthest from the centroid. Without solving for warping, the torsion constant of a polygon is estimated by Saint-Venant's approximation for solid sections, J≈min(Ip​, A4/(40Ip​)) with Ip​=Iy​+Iz​ (the exact factor of the formula is 4π2≈39.5). This estimate is crude for a hollow or thin section; a polygonal section entered in a model therefore receives by default the finite element solution of the next section.

20.4 Plastic properties#

The plastic section modulus is the first moment of the two halves of the section on either side of the plastic neutral axis, which divides the area into two equal parts. For a doubly symmetric section, this axis is the axis of symmetry, and the closed forms are, for the I and the box,

Wpl,yI​=btf​(h−tf​)+4tw​hw2​​,Wpl,ybox​=btf​(h−tf​)+2tw​hi2​​(20.8)

increased, for the rolled I, by 4As​(h/2−tf​−d) for the fillets. The tube uses the thin-wall approximation dm2​t, which differs from the exact value (d3−di3​)/6 by less than 0.1 % for t/d=1/25. keta does not compute the plastic modulus of a tee, of an arbitrary polygon or of a composite section: it remains zero, which means “unknown” for the checks that read it.

20.5 Saint-Venant problem#

For an arbitrary polygon, torsion and shear have no closed form. keta then solves by finite elements the three Laplace problems of Saint-Venant's theory, which give J, the shear areas, the shear centre, Iw​ and the shear stress fields. The formulation is that of Pilkey (Analysis and Design of Elastic Beams, 2002, chap. 5) and of Gruttmann and Wagner (“Shear correction factors in Timoshenko's beam theory for arbitrary shaped cross-sections”, Computational Mechanics 27, 2001). All the integrals are taken at the elastic centroid.

20.5.1 Torsion and warping function#

In uniform torsion, the section rotates by θ′x about the axis and warps by ux​=θ′ω(y,z). Equilibrium with no stress on the lateral surface gives a Neumann problem for the warping function:

Δω=0 in A,∂n∂ω​=zny​−ynz​ on the boundary(20.9)

In weak form, for any test function w, ∫A​∇w⋅∇ωdA=∫A​∇w⋅(z,−y)dA. ω is defined only up to a constant; keta fixes one node, which has no effect on the results since the right-hand side sums to zero. The torsion constant is obtained from the strain energy:

J=∫A​(y2+z2+y∂z∂ω​−z∂y∂ω​)dA=Iy​+Iz​−ωTft​(20.10)

where ft​ is the discrete right-hand side. The torsional shear stress is τ=(T/J)(∇ω+(−z,y)).

20.5.2 Bending and shear#

Under a shear force, Saint-Venant writes the shear field from two potentials ψy​ and ψz​, solutions of problems with the same operator as (20.9):

∫A​∇w⋅∇ψy​dA=∫A​[2ν​∇w⋅d+2(1+ν)w(Iy​y−Iyz​z)]dA(20.11)

with d=(Iy​r−Iyz​q, Iyz​r+Iy​q), r=y2−z2, q=2yz, and the analogous equation for ψz​, with source Iz​z−Iyz​y and compatibility term h=(−Iyz​r+Iz​q, −Iz​r−Iyz​q). The shear stress is

τ=ΔVy​​(∇ψy​−2ν​d)+ΔVz​​(∇ψz​−2ν​h),Δ=2(1+ν)(Iy​Iz​−Iyz2​)(20.12)

It depends on Poisson's ratio, because bending deforms the section in its plane (anticlastic effect); it satisfies the free-edge condition exactly, which the Jourawski formula τ=VS/(Ib) does not. The shear areas of the Timoshenko beam are those that reproduce the energy of this field:

Avy​=κy​Δ2​,κy​=∫A​​∇ψy​−2ν​d​2dA(20.13)

and likewise for Avz​; a section without symmetry also has a coupling area Avyz​. For ν=0, the rectangle gives Av​=5A/6; for ν=0.3, a 0.30 × 0.60 m rectangle gives Avz​=0.833A for a force parallel to the long side and Avy​=0.784A for the other direction. The solid circle and the tube have a closed form, derived from the Saint-Venant flexure energy:

Avcircle​=7+14ν+8ν26(1+ν)2​A,Avtube​=(7+14ν+8ν2)(1+m2)2+4(5+10ν+4ν2)m26(1+ν)2(1+m2)2A​,m=re​ri​​(20.14)

20.5.3 Shear centre and warping constant#

The shear centre is the point through which the shear force must pass so as not to twist the section. keta gives two definitions. The centre of elasticity, the one it uses, makes the shear field (20.12) free of torque; it is read from the coupling of the shear potentials with the torsion right-hand side:

yC​=Δ2ν​∫A​(Iz​y+Iyz​z)(y2+z2)dA−ftT​ψz​​,zC​=Δ2ν​∫A​(Iy​z+Iyz​y)(y2+z2)dA+ftT​ψy​​(20.15)

Trefftz's geometric centre cancels the sectorial products of the warping function, yT​=(Iyz​Iωz​−Iz​Iωy​)/(Iy​Iz​−Iyz2​), with Iωy​=∫yωdA. The two definitions differ only by the effect of Poisson's ratio. The warping constant is referred to the shear centre:

Iw​=∫A​ω2dA−A1​(∫A​ωdA)2−zC​Iωy​+yC​Iωz​(20.16)

keta also provides Vlasov's sectorial coordinate referred to the shear centre, with zero mean, at each node; it is this coordinate that gives the warping stress σw​=BωC​/Iw​ under a bimoment B.

On the channel of the figure, the finite element solution places the shear centre 37.2 mm from the midline of the web, on the side away from the flanges; the thin-walled theory described below gives 37.5 mm, that is, the classical formula e=3b2tf​/(6btf​+htw​). The warping constants differ by 0.7 % (79.3 and 78.8 · 10³ cm⁶) and the torsion constants by 2 % (16.3 and 16.6 cm⁴): the difference comes from the junctions and the free ends of the walls, which thin-walled theory idealises.

20.5.4 Discretisation#

The section is meshed with straight-sided six-node triangles. The shape functions are the second-degree polynomials in barycentric coordinates (η,ξ,ζ=1−η−ξ):

N1​=η(2η−1), N2​=ξ(2ξ−1), N3​=ζ(2ζ−1),N4​=4ηξ, N5​=4ξζ, N6​=4ηζ(20.17)

The Jacobian is constant over the element. The integrals are computed by the symmetric four-point Gauss rule, exact for polynomials of the third degree: the centroid, with weight −27/48 (a negative weight is correct for this rule), and the three points (0.6;0.2;0.2) with weight 25/48. The assembled matrix, symmetric and positive definite once one node is fixed, is factorised once by a sparse LDLT decomposition; the three right-hand sides (torsion, Vy​, Vz​) are solved on this factorisation.

The mesher is a Delaunay triangulation (Bowyer-Watson algorithm) of the boundary points, sampled at the target spacing h, and of an interior triangular lattice at the same spacing, kept at least 0.65h from any boundary. Under this condition, each boundary sub-edge is a Gabriel edge and belongs to the triangulation; the mesher checks this, as well as the equality of the meshed area and the Green area to within 10−9. If the check fails, because the spacing does not resolve the thinnest wall, it halves the spacing, at most three times, then stops with a message asking for a finer spacing. The interior nodes then undergo a pass of Laplacian smoothing, which moves a node only if the quality of the neighbouring triangles is improved. The default spacing is one twentieth of the largest dimension of the section; for the shear areas of the parametric shapes, keta takes min(L/40, tmin​/4). The computation is deterministic: the same section always gives the same mesh and the same properties, bit for bit.

Quadratic triangles converge quickly. On the 0.30 × 0.60 m rectangle, the difference of J from the series (20.1) is 3.7⋅10−5, 2.8⋅10−6 and 2.0⋅10−7 for spacings of 30, 15 and 7.5 mm, a convergence close to h4.

20.6 Transformed sections#

A section made of several materials (concrete and steel, two concretes) is described by regions: each mesh element whose centroid falls within the outline of a region takes its material, the last region listed prevailing; the other elements take the reference material, of index 0. The mesher respects the outline of the regions, which must therefore be drawn inside the section. All the integrals are weighted by ne​=Ee​/Eref​:

A∗=e∑​ne​Ae​,yG​=A∗1​∫nydA,Iy∗​=∫n(z−zG​)2dA(20.18)

and the warping problems likewise. The normal stress of an element is that of the transformed section multiplied by its ne​. In the shear potentials, the compatibility term νe​/2 takes the ratio of each material, whereas the equilibrium term keeps the 2(1+νˉ) of Δ, νˉ being the mean of the ratios weighted by n: the right-hand side thus still sums to zero and the result does not depend on the fixed node. The shear areas and J are values referred to Eref​; they are the true rigidities divided by Gref​ when all the materials have the same Poisson's ratio. On a square of side 1 whose upper half has a double modulus, keta finds A∗=1.5, zG​=1/12 and Iy∗​=0.11458, the hand values.

20.7 Stress field#

20.7.1 Stress at a point#

The internal forces of the bar are (N,Vy​,Vz​,T,My​,Mz​) and, in non-uniform torsion, the bimoment B. The normal stress, positive in tension and in coordinates referred to the centroid, is written without assuming principal axes:

σxx​=AN​+Iy​Iz​−Iyz2​My​(Iz​z−Iyz​y)−Mz​(Iy​y−Iyz​z)​+Iw​BωC​​(20.19)

A positive My​ puts the fibres of positive z in tension, a positive Mz​ compresses those of positive y (chapter 2). The shear stress is the sum of the torsional shear and the field (20.12); the von Mises stress is σvm​=σ2+3(τy2​+τz2​)​. The fields of the straight-sided triangles are polynomials evaluated directly at the requested point, without extrapolation. Since the normal stress is affine in (y,z), its extremes lie on the boundary, at a vertex of the outline: reading them at the boundary nodes, which include all the vertices, gives them exactly. The von Mises maximum, on the other hand, is taken at the mesh nodes; it is an approximate value. Principal stresses are not provided.

20.7.2 Stresses at the output sections of the bars#

After a structural analysis, keta returns at each output section of each bar the extremes of σ, the maximum of ∣τ∣ and that of von Mises. Since the field is linear in the six internal forces, the warping problem is solved only once per section geometry and Poisson's ratio, and six unit nodal fields are stored; the field of an output section is their combination:

s(node)=k=1∑6​ak​sk​(node),(ak​)=(N,Vy​,Vz​,My​,Mz​,T)(20.20)

The nodal value is the average of the elements that meet at that node, the shear being discontinuous from one element to the next. The extremes are sought over a set of candidate nodes: all the boundary nodes, which make the extremes of σ exact, and the nodes that govern each unit action and each pair of unit actions of either sign. The maxima of τ and of von Mises of a combined field may lie elsewhere: they are maxima over this set, not bounds. The parametric shapes are then drawn as polygons (circle with 48 sides, fillets of rolled sections with 8 chords per quarter circle, so that the section used for stresses has the same stiffness as that of the analysis). The mesh, a constrained Delaunay triangulation refined by Ruppert's method (minimum angle of 30°), aims at 1200, 3000 or 9000 elements per section depending on the chosen fineness; the mesher of the previous section serves as a fallback. A section without geometry, or whose solution has failed, falls back on the named stress points of its shape (normal stress only), and keta reports it with the reason.

20.8 Thin walls#

Open sections and bridge boxes lend themselves to thin-walled theory, faster than finite elements and without a mesh. The section is a plane graph: the nodes are points of the midline, the edges are straight walls.

20.8.1 Model and properties#

A wall of length L, thickness t, unit direction e=(ey​,ez​) and midpoint (ym​,zm​) is a rectangle centred on its midline; its area and second moments of area are exact:

As​=tL,Iy,s​=As​(zm2​+12L2ez2​+t2ey2​​),Iyz,s​=As​(ym​zm​+12(L2−t2)ey​ez​​)(20.21)

The material that overlaps at the junctions is counted twice: this is the classical idealisation, an effect of order t/L, and the reason why the J of a rolled section computed in this way is lower than that of the finite elements (neither fillet nor junction stiffening). The elastic section moduli are taken at the ends of the midline.

20.8.2 Wall of linearly varying thickness#

A wall may have a thickness that varies linearly from ta​ at node a to tb​ at node b, symmetrically on either side of the midline (thickened box web, haunch). All the formulae remain valid with t=t(s) under the integrals: the second moments of area are polynomial, the strip term becomes ∫t3ds/12, and

∫0L​tds​=Ltb​−ta​ln(tb​/ta​)​,31​∫0L​t3ds=12L(ta​+tb​)(ta2​+tb2​)​(20.22)

The rational and logarithmic integrals (flow energy, no-twist conditions, sectorial moments) are computed by an 8-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature on segments of the wall whose thickness ratio remains below 1.5: the result is exact to within rounding.

20.8.3 Multi-cell Bredt torsion#

The independent cells are the fundamental cycles of a spanning tree of the graph: with V nodes and E walls of a connected graph, there are n=E−V+1 of them. Working in this basis of the cycle space is equivalent to working on the faces of the drawing, without needing to know the drawing. For a unit rotation Gθ′=1, the circulating flows xk​ of the cells are solutions of the compatibility system

Mx=2A,Mkl​=s∑​ck​(s)cl​(s)ts​Ls​​,J=2k∑​xk​Ak​+open∑​3bt3​(20.23)

where ck​(s)=±1 states whether wall s belongs to cycle k and in which direction, and Ak​ is the area of the cell. For a single cell, (20.2) is recovered. The walls that belong to no cycle (cantilevers of a box, flanges of an open section) carry the open Saint-Venant term; those of a cell carry the closed term alone. Under a torque T, keta gives the flow of each cell, the flow and the stress q/t of each closed wall, and the Saint-Venant stress Gθ′t of the open walls. A box with midlines of 0.60 × 1.00 m (flanges of 0.60 m and 30 mm, webs of 1.00 m and 20 mm) has J=1.0286⋅10−2 m4, the value of (20.2); an added central web leaves J unchanged: the two symmetric cells have the same flow and the central web carries none.

20.8.4 Shear flow#

The longitudinal equilibrium of a wall element gives the variation of the flow q=τt along the midline:

dsdq​=−(Kz​z+Ky​y)t,Kz​=Iy​Iz​−Iyz2​Vz​Iz​−Vy​Iyz​​,Ky​=Iy​Iz​−Iyz2​Vy​Iy​−Vz​Iyz​​(20.24)

that is, the familiar formula q=VS(s)/I in principal axes. keta takes as unknowns the flows at the start of the E walls. The V−1 independent nodal equilibria and the n no-twist conditions of the cells (a force applied at the shear centre twists nothing) form a square system, which reduces to the classical accumulation from the free end to the root for an open section, and to the “cut the cells, then restore compatibility” method for a closed section. The shear areas come from the energy of the flow:

Av​=∮tq2​dsV2​(20.25)

20.8.5 Sectorial coordinate and shear centre#

The sectorial coordinate referred to a pole P is integrated along the walls:

dω=(y−yP​)dz−(z−zP​)dy−tq1​​ds(20.26)

where q1​ is the unit torsional flow of (20.23). The corrective term is zero for an open section; for a closed section, it makes ω single-valued around each cell, since its integral over a cycle is 2Ak​−∮q1​/tds=0. The shear centre S cancels the sectorial products:

[Iyz​Iy​​−Iz​−Iyz​​](yP​−yS​zP​−zS​​)=−(Iωy​Iωz​​)(20.27)

and, once ω is referred to this pole and brought to zero mean, Iw​=∫ωS2​dA and σw​=BωS​/Iw​. In a wall of constant thickness of an open section, ω is linear; in a cell with a wall of linearly varying thickness, it follows ∫ds/t and becomes logarithmic. For the channel of the previous figure, the thin-walled computation gives Iw​=78.75⋅103 cm6, the closed formula tf​b3h2(3btf​+2htw​)/(12(6btf​+htw​)) to within 6⋅10−7.

20.8.6 Midline derived from the outline#

The midline of an outline cannot be found without help: pairing the two faces of a wall is ambiguous at the junctions. The user states how the points of the outline group into walls, in one of two ways. By quadrilaterals: each wall is given by four points p,q,r,s taken in order, p→q and s→r being its two faces and ps, qr its ends. The midline joins the midpoints of the ends, and the thickness at an end is that end projected onto the normal n to the midline,

ta​=​(p−s)⋅n​,tb​=​(q−r)⋅n​(20.28)

which gives the true thickness of a mitred corner, not its diagonal. End midpoints closer than a tolerance (by default 10−6 of the extent of the outline) are merged into a single node. By nodes and branches: a node is the list of outline points gathered at a junction, a branch connects two nodes. The centre of the points of each node gives the axis of each branch; the spread of the points of a node across this axis gives the thickness of the branch at that end and the point of its midline; finally, each node is moved to the least-squares intersection of the midlines of its branches, the mitre point of a corner. When the thicknesses of the two ends differ, the wall has a linearly varying thickness. A wall without a determinate midline is refused: zero length or thickness, or faces of a quadrilateral whose lengths differ by more than half of the longer one.

20.9 Inclined chords: the Résal effect#

In a beam of variable depth, the inclined chord (soffit of a haunched span, slab of a box of variable depth) carries its axial force along its own line. The shear force of the section, the resultant of the stresses on the facet, therefore includes the transverse component of this force, and the web carries only the remainder: this is the Résal effect (BPEL 91 art. 7.1.2; EN 1992-1-1 § 6.2.1(1), terms Vccd​ and Vtd​). A chord of area Af​, with centroid (yf​,zf​) referred to that of the section, whose line has the slopes dyf​/dx and dzf​/dx, carries the force Nf​=σf​Af​, where σf​ is the linear stress of the section at that point:

σf​=AN​+Iy​My​zf​​−Iz​Mz​yf​​,Vz,web​=Vz​−f∑​Nf​dxdzf​​,Vy,web​=Vy​−f∑​Nf​dxdyf​​(20.29)

The share carried by the chords is linear in the forces of the section, with factors that depend only on the section:

Vz​−Vz,web​=kz​⋅(N,My​,Mz​),kz​=f∑​Af​dxdzf​​(A1​, Iy​zf​​, −Iz​yf​​)(20.30)

For two equal chords a distance h apart, without a web, the bottom chord inclined at α, under a moment M alone that puts the top chord in tension, the bottom chord carries Nf​=−M/h; if it rises by α as x increases, (20.29) leaves the web V+(M/h)tanα. keta recovers this value (588.16 kN for Vz​=500 kN, My​=1 MN·m, h=2 m and α = 10°). keta returns raw, signed values: it is up to the user to read whether the effect relieves the web (a haunch that deepens towards the larger moment) or loads it.

20.10 Transfer of the properties to the bar#

A bar reads A, Iy​, Iz​, J, Avy​, Avz​ and, in non-uniform torsion, Iw​. The shear areas depend on Poisson's ratio, which belongs to the material and not to the section. When a bar receives a parametric section whose areas are still the provisional values of its shape, keta computes the energy-based areas (20.13) for the ν of the material of the bar: closed forms (20.14) for the circle and the tube, Saint-Venant solution of the rectangle in closed form, finite element solution on the actual outline (fillets included) for the other shapes. A section used with two materials of different ratios is duplicated, each copy carrying the areas of its ν:

GAv​1​ in the flexibility of the element,G=2(1+ν)E​,Av​=Av​(ν)(20.31)

Areas entered by hand are kept; a zero area means a bar without shear deformation (Euler-Bernoulli). A global option reverts to the classical factors 5/6 and 0.9 of the shape formulae.

When the Saint-Venant problem has been solved for a section (systematically for a polygon entered in a model), the result is attached to the section and shared by all the bars that use it: it replaces J, Avy​ and Avz​. The area and the bending second moments of area remain those of the closed forms or of the Green integrals, which are already exact. The warping constant of a parametric I section is the closed form of the table; for another section, it is given to the bar by the non-uniform torsion analysis (chapter 19). Composite bars made of several parts cast at different dates (steel girder and slab, box cast in several pours) are sections transformed part by part, A∗=∑ni​Ai​, Iy∗​=∑ni​(Iy,i​+Ai​(zi​−zG​)2), J∗=∑(Gi​/Gref​)Ji​, carried by an element on rigid arms up to their centroid; their treatment is that of staged construction (chapter 10).

20.11 Scope#

  • the section is in one piece; several disjoint regions are refused by the warping computation, which has no meaning component by component about a common centre;
  • curved edges are polygons: the J of a circle divided into n sides carries the geometric error of the inscribed polygon, of the order of 2π2/(3n2) on the area;
  • a bar polygon must be given in its principal axes (product of inertia below 5 % of the larger second moment of area); keta does not rotate the section;
  • the plastic section modulus is computed only for the rectangle, the circle, the tube, the I and the box;
  • a composite section is weighted by the Young's moduli alone: J and the shear areas are the true rigidities only if the Poisson's ratios are equal;
  • thin-walled theory knows only one material per graph and neglects effects of order t/L, fillets and shear lag;
  • the non-uniform torsion of the bar handles only doubly symmetric sections (chapter 19), even though the shear centre and Iw​ are computed for any section; the monosymmetry coefficients are not computed.
