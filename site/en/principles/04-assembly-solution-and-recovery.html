Chapter 4 Assembly, solution and recovery

This chapter follows a linear static analysis from start to finish: the numbering of the unknowns and the elimination of the supports, the assembly of the sparse stiffness matrix, the treatment of rigid links and linear relations, the factorisation of the matrix and its use as a diagnostic, then the passage from the nodal displacements to the quantities the engineer works with: reactions, internal forces and displacements along the bars, stresses. The same tools serve the other analyses, which call on them at each of their steps: chapter 15 adds the mass, chapter 17 the geometric stiffness and the Newton iterations, chapter 10 the succession of stages. The elements whose matrices are assembled are described in chapters 3 (bars), 6 (supports and elastic links), 24 and 25 (shells and continuum elements).

4.1 Assumptions#

  • the problem is linear: the stiffness matrix depends neither on the displacements nor on the loads, and the results of several cases can be superposed;
  • all the load cases of an analysis act on the same structure: same elements, same supports, same links; they therefore share the same matrix;
  • supports are bilateral. Unilateral and capped supports, which make each case nonlinear, are dealt with in chapter 6;
  • rigid links are exact (no penalty stiffness) and rotations in them are small.

4.2 Numbering and elimination of the supports#

The global displacement vector stores the six components of each node in the order in which the nodes were created: component c of the node of rank n occupies position 6n+c. The degrees of freedom restrained by a rigid support are removed from this vector; the others, called free, are renumbered in the same order. Partitioning the unknowns into free f and restrained s, equilibrium reads

[Kff​Ksf​​Kfs​Kss​​][uf​uˉs​​]=[Ff​Fs​+Rs​​]⟹Kff​uf​=Ff​−Kfs​uˉs​(4.1)

where uˉs​ is zero except for an imposed support displacement (settlement, support rotation) and Rs​ denotes the reactions. Only the matrix Kff​ is assembled and factorised; it does not depend on the imposed displacements, which move to the right-hand side. The product Kfs​uˉs​ is computed element by element, without forming Kfs​. A displacement can be imposed only on a degree of freedom restrained by a rigid support, and only once per case: otherwise the calculation stops.

Elastic supports add their stiffness to the diagonal of Kff​, or a full 6×6 matrix to the block of the node for a coupled elastic support (chapter 6). Their degrees of freedom remain unknowns.

4.2.1 Automatic restraints#

A degree of freedom that receives strictly no stiffness from any element, any elastic support or any link is eliminated as if it were restrained, and reported in the list of automatic restraints:

e∑​kii(e)​=0andkiisupport​=0⟹ui​ eliminated(4.2)

Since the diagonal terms of the element matrices are positive or zero, the sum is zero only if no element restrains this degree of freedom. The typical case is that of the rotations of a node that carries only truss bars or cables, or of an isolated node. The criterion is an exact zero: it does not detect a stiffness that is non-zero but insufficient (for example a rotation restrained in a single direction that is not a global axis). Such cases are caught by the pivot check described further on. No reaction is returned on an automatic restraint, since it transmits no force.

4.3 Assembly#

The global matrix is the sum of the contributions of all the elements, each placed in the rows and columns of its free degrees of freedom:

Kff​=e∑​AeT​Ke​Ae​+Ksupports​+ℓ∑​BℓT​kℓ​Bℓ​,Ff​=AT(Fnodes​+e∑​AeT​TeT​f~e0​)(4.3)

Ke​ is the matrix of the element in global axes, with hinges condensed and rigid arms and elastic foundation included (chapter 3); Bℓ​ and kℓ​ are the deformation operator and the stiffness of an elastic link between two nodes (chapter 6); f~e0​ are the condensed equivalent nodal forces of the span loads. Since a node is coupled only to its neighbours, the matrix is sparse: keta stores only its non-zero terms, column by column. The order of assembly is fixed (bars, then shells, continuum elements, elastic supports and links, each in order of creation), which makes the matrix identical, bit for bit, from one calculation to the next.

Cables cannot be assembled into a linear matrix: a model that contains them is refused by the linear analyses, which point to the cable analysis of chapter 7 or to replacing the cables with bars of equivalent stiffness.

4.4 Rigid links and linear relations#

Three types of kinematic conditions relate degrees of freedom to one another: rigid links (a master node and slave nodes that follow its rigid-body motion), general linear relations and skew supports (chapter 6). All are eliminated by substitution, with no penalty stiffness and no multiplier in the system. For a rigid link, each linked component of a slave s is expressed in terms of its master m by the rigid-body relation:

us​=um​+θm​×(xs​−xm​),θs​=θm​(4.4)

A mask restricts the link to certain global components (a floor diaphragm links only uX​, uY​ and θZ​, for example). A general linear relation ∑k​ck​uk​=0 eliminates its first term, u1​=−∑k>1​(ck​/c1​)uk​. A skew support restrains an arbitrary direction; the global component with the largest coefficient that is still free is eliminated. Chains (a slave that is the master of another) are resolved step by step; a component linked twice, a supported slave component or a cycle of links is refused.

Together these relations read u=Tur​+u0​, where ur​ collects the degrees of freedom not eliminated and u0​ the part due to the imposed displacements of supported masters. The system solved is

TTKTur​=TT(F−Ku0​)(4.5)

The matrix and the forces remain assembled in the unreduced space, which leaves assembly and recovery unchanged; only the factorisation works on the reduced matrix. The forces transmitted by a link are deduced from the out-of-balance Ku−F at the degrees of freedom it eliminates: that of a slave is transferred to the degrees of freedom of its master, and becomes a reaction if the master is supported. For a linear relation, the multiplier μ such that Ku−F=μc is returned as the force of the relation. Rigid links are accepted by linear statics, by modal, spectral and linear time-history analyses (starting from rest), by influence lines, buckling, staged construction and large-displacement static analysis. The analyses that do not handle them refuse them with a message: nonlinear dynamics, staged construction in large displacements and plasticity of continuum elements, for example.

4.5 Factorisation#

4.5.1 LDLᵀ decomposition#

The reduced matrix is symmetric. keta factorises it in the form

PKPT=LDLT(4.6)

where P is a permutation of the unknowns, L a unit lower triangular matrix and D a diagonal matrix, whose terms dk​ are the pivots. The factorisation is performed without numerical pivoting: the elimination order is fixed in advance by P, and each pivot may be negative. This form suits the positive definite matrices of statics, and also the indefinite matrices of stability and eigenvalue analyses, whose negative pivots it counts. Solving a load case is then a forward substitution Ly=PF, a division by D and a back substitution.

4.5.2 Reordering#

The cost of the factorisation depends on the elimination order. Eliminating a node couples all of its remaining neighbours to one another: the zero terms of K that become non-zero in L form the fill-in. keta chooses the order by the approximate minimum degree (AMD) algorithm, which at each step eliminates the least connected unknown. Beyond 10,000 unknowns, it also computes a nested dissection order: the graph is cut by a separator (a level of a breadth-first search from a pseudo-peripheral vertex, the smallest level that leaves between 35 % and 65 % of the vertices on each side), the two halves are treated recursively down to 64 vertices, and the separators are eliminated last. For each of the two orders, keta computes the factorisation work by a symbolic elimination,

W=j∑​cj2​(4.7)

where cj​ is the number of non-zero terms in column j of L, and keeps the dissection if it requires less than 90 % of the work of the AMD order. The graphs of decks and frames, which are nearly planar, generally stay with the AMD order; space frames and solid meshes benefit from dissection. Both algorithms are deterministic.

4.5.3 Simplicial and supernodal factorisation#

Below 10,000 unknowns, the factorisation is simplicial: column by column. Above, it is supernodal and multifrontal. Columns with the same structure below the diagonal are grouped into supernodes; each supernode forms a dense frontal matrix, which receives the terms of K and the update matrices of its children in the elimination tree, and is then factorised by dense block operations. Partitioning the frontal matrix between the columns of the supernode (index 1) and the remaining rows (index 2),

F11​=L11​D1​L11T​,L21​=F21​L11−T​D1−1​,U=F22​−L21​D1​L21T​(4.8)

the pivot block being factorised densely without permutation, the panel L21​ obtained by a triangular solve and the update matrix U, passed to the parent, by matrix products. The computation is the same as the simplicial factorisation, up to rounding errors; it is merely organised around dense matrix products. Independent subtrees and the 256-row blocks of a large front can be processed in parallel, in a fixed order of operations: the result is identical, bit for bit, whatever the number of cores.

4.5.4 Reuse of the factorisation#

All the load cases of a static analysis are solved with a single factorisation: the right-hand sides are stored in a matrix and solved together,

[u(1) ⋯ u(n)]=K−1[F(1) ⋯ F(n)](4.9)

by one forward and one back substitution per case. Analyses that factorise similar matrices several times (staged construction, nonlinear iterations, influence lines) reuse what can be reused, at three levels: if the matrix is identical, term by term and bit for bit, to the previous one, the factorisation is kept; if only its structure is identical, the symbolic analysis (order and structure of L) is kept and only the numbers are recomputed; otherwise, everything is redone. The comparison is made on the arrays of the matrix themselves, not on a hash.

4.6 Inertia and mechanism checks#

4.6.1 Sylvester's law of inertia#

The factorisation also provides a diagnostic at no extra cost. By Sylvester's law of inertia, a symmetric matrix and the diagonal matrix of an LDLT decomposition have the same numbers of positive, zero and negative eigenvalues:

ν−​(K)=card{k:dk​<0},ν0​(K)=card{k:dk​=0}(4.10)

A stable structure has a positive definite matrix: all its pivots are positive. A zero pivot reveals a mechanism; the factorisation then stops with an error. Stability and large-displacement analyses also count the negative pivots of K+Kg​ to detect that a critical load has been passed (chapter 17); applied to K−σM, the same rule counts the natural modes whose squared circular frequency is below σ (chapter 15).

4.6.2 Ratio of pivot to diagonal term#

Because of rounding errors, the pivot of a mechanism is often not exactly zero: three aligned hinges, a chain of bars free to rotate, a node rotation restrained in an oblique direction only. The factorisation then succeeds, and the solution would return huge displacements of arbitrary sign. In linear statics, keta therefore compares each pivot with the diagonal term akk​ of the assembled matrix for the same unknown, and refuses the calculation if

kmin​∣akk​∣∣dk​∣​<10−13(4.11)

The ratio dk​/akk​ measures the part of the stiffness of unknown k that remains when the unknowns eliminated before it are free to adjust; for a mechanism, only rounding remains, of the order of 10−16. A sound model stays far above the threshold, unless two stiffnesses that follow each other in the same chain differ by more than thirteen orders of magnitude, a case that is also refused because the solution would no longer have any significant digit. The error message gives the value of the ratio, but does not locate the degrees of freedom involved.

The check is made on the matrix, before any solution: a mechanism is refused even if the loads do not excite it. This is the case, for example, of the end node of an inclined bar hinged in bending at that node and whose torsion is retained, when the node is connected to nothing but translational supports: the torsion of the bar restrains the rotation of the node only about the axis of the bar, and the perpendicular rotation, which is not zero component by component, is restrained by nothing. The ratio is then about 3⋅10−16. The torsion must also be released at that end (the rotation of the node then receives no stiffness at all and is eliminated automatically), or the rotation restrained by a support.

Note

The threshold applies to the ratio of stiffnesses in series, not to their absolute difference. A steel bar connected to a support of stiffness 1025 N/m passes the check, because the pivot of that support remains equal to its diagonal term. On the other hand, two truss bars in series whose axial stiffnesses differ by a factor of 1011 give a displacement with a relative error of 3⋅10−6, the relative error being of the order of rounding multiplied by the contrast; with a contrast of 1014, the calculation is refused. An “infinitely stiff” bar is better modelled by a rigid link, with no fictitious stiffness, than by an inordinate modulus.

4.7 Reactions#

Once the displacements are known, the reactions are obtained at the supported nodes as the out-of-balance between the forces that the elements exert on the node and the loads applied to it:

R=e∑​AeT​Ke​ue​−Fnodes​−e∑​AeT​TeT​f~e0​(4.12)

The last term removes the equivalent nodal forces of the span loads: the reaction of a simply supported beam under a uniform load is indeed qL/2. R is the force that the support exerts on the structure, in global axes, on the six components of the node; on a free component of a partially supported node, it is zero to within rounding. On a free component restrained by an elastic support, it equals −ku, the force of the spring on the structure. The forces transmitted by rigid links to supported masters are included; skew supports return their reaction in their own axes (chapter 6). Modelling restraints, which do not represent a bearing (out-of-plane degrees of freedom of a plane model, for example), return no reaction. By construction, the sum of the reactions balances the sum of the applied loads to within rounding, which is the first check of a model.

4.8 Internal forces between the nodes#

4.8.1 End forces#

For each bar, the local end displacements are uℓ​=Te​ue​, completed by the released displacements (3.15). The end forces are then

f=kuℓ​−f0(4.13)

with the matrix k of the bar alone (not condensed, without the elastic foundation, whose reaction is returned separately as a soil pressure) and the sum f0 of the equivalent nodal forces of all its span loads.

4.8.2 Equilibrium of the segment#

The internal forces at a section of abscissa x are obtained by writing the equilibrium of the segment [0,x] under the end forces at i and the loads it actually carries, without any interpolation. With the convention of chapter 2, and writing px​,qy​,qz​ for the local distributed loads (point loads appearing in them as Dirac distributions) and mx​,my​,mz​ for the distributed and point moments:

N(x)Vy​(x)Vz​(x)​=−f1​−∫0x​px​dξ,=−f2​−∫0x​qy​dξ,=−f3​−∫0x​qz​dξ,​T(x)Mz​(x)My​(x)​=−f4​−∫0x​mx​dξ,=−f6​+f2​x+∫0x​(x−ξ)qy​dξ−∫0x​mz​dξ,=−f5​−f3​x−∫0x​(x−ξ)qz​dξ−∫0x​my​dξ​(4.14)

The integrals are computed in closed form for uniform, linear and parabolic loads. A point load located exactly at the abscissa of an output section is counted in the segment [0,x]: the shear force returned is the one just after the load. The only exception is the section x=L, which returns the value just before a load placed on the end, since that load acts on the node. These internal forces satisfy equilibrium at every section and do not depend on the number of output sections: the moment of a simply supported beam under a uniform load is qx(L−x)/2 at every section, even if the beam is represented by a single bar. On a 10 m beam carrying a linear load from 10 to 30 kN/m between 2 and 7 m and a 50 kN force at 4.5 m, the moment returned at 3.7 m, 272.108 kN·m, agrees with the statics calculation to within 10−12.

Bars with variable cross-section and bars on exact elastic foundation follow the same principle, with their own end forces; the bar on foundation also derives the soil reaction along the bar (chapter 6).

4.9 Displacements between the nodes#

4.9.1 Integration of the curvature#

The displacements at the output sections are obtained by integrating the strains of the bar, known at every section from the internal forces (4.14) and the constitutive laws of chapter 3. In each bending plane, the rotation of the cross-section and the deflection satisfy

dxdθz​​=EIz​Mz​​−κz∗​(x),dxdv​=θz​+GAvy​Vy​​(4.15)

and likewise in the xz plane, with dθy​/dx=My​/EIy​−κy∗​ and dw/dx=−θy​+Vz​/GAvz​; κ∗ is the imposed curvature (thermal, shrinkage, creep). The double integration starts from the start of the bar; its two constants are fixed by the transverse displacements of the two ends, which also suits hinged bars, whose end rotations are recomputed. The rotation of the cross-section returned is the rotation of end i plus the integral of the curvature; the shear strain is not a rotation and does not enter it. The axial displacement is obtained in the same way from u′=N/EA+ε0∗​, and the twist from θx′​=T/GJ+κx∗​, anchored on its two end values. Dislocations add their jumps at their abscissa.

4.9.2 Numerical method#

Each interval between two output sections is cut at the point loads and at the ends of the distributed loads it contains. On each piece, the curvature is a polynomial of degree at most 4 (the moment under a parabolic load), and the integration

v(b)=v(a)+(b−a)v′(a)+∫ab​(b−s)κ(s)ds+∫ab​GAv​V(s)​ds,v′(b)=v′(a)+∫ab​κ(s)ds(4.16)

is carried out by a three-point Gauss quadrature, exact up to degree 5. The axial displacement and the twist are integrated by Simpson's rule on the same pieces, which is exact for their integrands. The displacements are therefore exact at every section, for all the loads of chapter 3: a simply supported beam represented by a single bar gives the deflection (1.3) at midspan, to machine precision. For a bar with variable cross-section, the integrand is no longer polynomial: each piece is integrated by an adaptive Gauss quadrature, subdivided as long as two evaluations differ by more than 10−14 in relative terms (24 levels at most). Truss bars interpolate the displacements of their ends linearly.

4.10 Stresses at the stress points#

Each parametric cross-section carries named stress points: the four corners and the centre of a rectangle, the flange tips and the web of an I-section or a box, the four cardinal points of a circle or a tube, the vertices of a polygonal section. At each of them, keta returns the normal stress (2.15), an estimate of the shear stress and the von Mises equivalent stress:

τ=ϕy​Avy​Vy​​+ϕz​Avz​Vz​​+JTr​,σVM​=σ2+3τ2​(4.17)

where ϕy​, ϕz​ equal 1 at the points where the corresponding shear force is carried (centre of the rectangle, web, points of the circle lying on the neutral axis of the bending considered) and 0 elsewhere, and r is the distance from the point to the centroid. This estimate is an average value: V/Av​ underestimates the maximum of the actual distribution (1.5 V/A at the centre of a rectangle, against 1.2 V/A with Av​=5A/6), and the torsion term Tr/J is exact only for circular sections. For the exact stresses at any point of an arbitrary cross-section, chapter 20 solves the Saint-Venant torsion and flexure problems on the meshed cross-section.

4.11 Output sections#

Internal forces, displacements and stresses are returned at the output sections of each bar. By default, there are eleven, equally spaced, ends included:

xk​=n−1k​L,k=0,…,n−1,n=11(4.18)

The number can be changed for all bars, and each bar can receive its own list of abscissae, sorted, in metres from its start. Since the internal forces and displacements are exact at every section, the choice of output sections affects only where the results are read, not their value: it is enough to place a section at the points of interest (under a point load, at a change of cross-section, at the point of maximum moment). Combinations of cases (chapter 5) are formed section by section and therefore assume the same output sections for all cases.

4.12 Scope and limits#

  • the solution assumes a stable structure: a mechanism, even unloaded, and a contrast of stiffnesses in series greater than 1013 are refused before any solution;
  • a displacement imposed on a degree of freedom that is not restrained, or imposed twice in a case, is refused;
  • cables, which are nonlinear, are refused by the linear analyses (chapter 7); rigid and elastic links are accepted only by the analyses that handle them;
  • a model with unilateral or capped supports is solved case by case by an active-set method (chapter 6): its results cannot be superposed and combinations are refused for it;
  • the shear stress at the stress points is an estimate; exact stresses are the subject of chapter 20.
