Chapter 1 Introduction

keta is a finite-element kernel for framed structures and bridges. It takes the description of a structure (nodes, bars and their cross-sections, materials, supports, links, load cases) and returns the displacements of the nodes, the support reactions, the internal forces and displacements along the bars, and the stresses at chosen points of the cross-sections. This document sets out its principles: the mechanical problem posed by each analysis, the assumptions made, the equations solved, the numerical method, the results provided and their limits. It is written for the engineer who wants to know where the numbers come from, so as to interpret them, check them by hand and choose the appropriate modelling. How to describe a structure and run an analysis is the subject of the user manual and is not repeated here.

This first chapter sets the framework: the objects the kernel handles, the displacement method on which every analysis rests, the form of the equations solved and the organisation of the document. The conventions for axes, signs and units are given in detail in chapter 2.

1.1 General assumptions#

Unless the chapter dealing with a particular analysis states otherwise, the following assumptions hold throughout the document:

  • the structure is described by nodes connected by elements; each node carries six degrees of freedom, three translations and three rotations, expressed in a right-handed global system whose Z axis points vertically upwards;
  • displacements and rotations are small: equilibrium is written on the initial geometry and rotations combine as vectors. Chapters 7, 16 and 17 remove this assumption (cables, nonlinear dynamics, stability and large displacements), as do the large-displacement variants of staged construction and non-uniform torsion (chapters 10 and 19);
  • materials are linear elastic. The time-dependent behaviour of concrete (chapter 9), the nonlinearity of bars and cross-sections (chapters 18 and 21), fire (chapter 23) and the plasticity of shells and continuum elements (chapters 24 and 25) are covered by specific models; nonlinear supports, links and soils are described in chapter 6;
  • loads are applied without dynamic effect, except in the dynamic analyses (chapters 15 and 16);
  • quantities are expressed in the base units of the International System (metre, newton, kilogram, second, radian), with no internal conversion; only the ages and durations of concrete are counted in days, as in the standards that define its laws.

1.2 The objects of the model#

The kernel was designed for framed structures: beams, columns, piers, decks modelled by a line of bars or by a grillage. The bars are complemented by the elements a bridge needs around them: cables, shells for slabs and deck slabs, continuum elements for massive parts and details. Loads are applied to nodes (forces and moments), to bars (distributed, point, thermal loads and imposed strains), to surfaces (pressures, hydrostatic loads) and to supports (imposed displacements). They are grouped into load cases, which are then combined (chapter 5).

1.3 The displacement method#

Every analysis in keta rests on the displacement method. The unknowns are the displacements and rotations of the nodes, collected in a vector u. Each element relates the displacements of its nodes, ue​, to the forces it exerts on them through its stiffness matrix ke​; the loads applied between the nodes are replaced by equivalent nodal forces fe0​. The end forces of the element are

fe​=ke​ue​−fe0​(1.1)

The equilibrium of each node, under the forces of the elements meeting there and the loads applied to it, gives the global system

Ku=F,K=e∑​AeT​ke​Ae​,F=Fnodes​+e∑​AeT​fe0​(1.2)

where Ae​ extracts from u the degrees of freedom of the element, expressed in its own axes. The same system expresses the stationarity of the total potential energy Π(u)=21​uTKu−uTF: for a stable structure, K is symmetric positive definite and the solution is the minimum of Π. Supports remove the restrained degrees of freedom from the system; rigid links and linear relations eliminate others. Once u is known, the reactions are recovered, then, bar by bar, the internal forces and displacements at each output section.

For a straight prismatic bar, keta uses the exact stiffness matrix of the chosen beam theory and exact equivalent nodal forces (chapter 3). The nodal displacements obtained are then those of beam theory, with no discretisation error, and the internal forces between the nodes are recomputed from the equilibrium of the bar under its actual load (chapter 4). For a simply supported beam of span L represented by a single bar, under a uniform load q, one reads at every section, in absolute value (the span moment is negative in the convention of chapter 2),

M(x)=2qx(L−x)​,w(2L​)=384EI5qL4​+8GAv​qL2​(1.3)

the second term of the deflection being the shear contribution. A single bar between two nodes is therefore enough in linear statics, prismatic or with variable cross-section, whatever distributed load it carries. Subdividing a bar is useful only where the model itself is approximate: distributed mass in dynamics, consistent geometric stiffness in stability, large displacements, an elastic foundation where the exact beam-on-elastic-foundation element does not apply (chapter 6).

1.5 Three design rules#

Three choices guide the whole kernel and explain most of the behaviour observed in use.

The exact solution of the chosen model. When a closed-form solution exists for the chosen mechanical model, keta uses it rather than an approximation: exact stiffness matrix of the prismatic Timoshenko beam, internal forces between the nodes obtained from equilibrium, elastic catenary computed from its closed-form formulae, integrals evaluated by quadratures that integrate them without error. The approximation, where there is one, is that of the mechanical model and not that of the discretisation, and the text points it out. The main discretisation approximations that remain are listed below; they are what justifies, where needed, subdividing the bars.

QuantityApproximationChapter
mass of the barsconsistent (Hermite functions) or lumped matrix3, 15
geometric stiffnessconsistent matrix; at second order, optional stability functions, exact with one element per bar17
elastic foundation under a barconsistent matrix, except in linear statics for a prismatic Euler-Bernoulli bar without release (exact element)3, 6
stiffness of a bar with variable cross-section16-point Gauss quadrature, exact for the parabolic law3
shells and continuum elementsstandard finite elements, convergent under mesh refinement24, 25

An explicit refusal rather than a silent approximation. When a combination of options is not handled, the calculation stops with an error that names it: a parabolic load in an analysis that can only handle linear loads, a cable in a linear analysis, a mechanism. It is never silently replaced by a simplified calculation, and an input or a result that is not a finite number stops the calculation instead of being returned.

Reproducible calculations. The same model gives the same numbers, bit for bit, from one run to the next and whatever the number of cores used: parallel operations are split into fixed blocks and their results stored in a fixed order. Two versions of the software can therefore be compared digit by digit.

1.6 Units and axes in brief#

The kernel knows only the base units of the International System. Input is read and results are returned in these units; there is no hidden conversion factor and no default unit of any other kind.

The acceleration due to gravity is g=9.80665 m/s2, directed along −Z by default. Each bar carries local axes: x runs from the start node to the end node, y and z are the principal axes of the cross-section. The axial force is positive in tension. Chapter 2 gives these conventions in detail.
