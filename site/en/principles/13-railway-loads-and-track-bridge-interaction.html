Chapter 13 Railway loads and track-bridge interaction

A railway bridge is loaded by trains whose vertical loads are heavy and concentrated, whose speed can excite the structure into resonance, and whose horizontal forces (braking, traction) pass from the rail to the deck through the ballast. The continuous welded rail, for its part, is restrained by the ballast along its whole length: it interacts with the deck, which expands and deflects beneath it. This chapter describes what keta computes for these three questions: the load models of EN 1991-2 section 6 and their factors, the dynamic response of a deck to the passage of a train at a given speed, and the track-bridge interaction analysis of UIC leaflet 774-3. The search for the most adverse placing on an influence line or surface is that of chapter 12; the time integration of the equations of motion is set out in chapter 15, and the service trains for fatigue in chapter 14.

13.1 Assumptions#

  • For vertical and horizontal loads: linear elastic structure in small displacements, static loads; the dynamic effect of the load models is carried by the factor Φ, a multiplier computed separately.
  • For dynamics: linear structure described by its eigenmodes, modal damping, train of point forces of fixed direction moving at constant speed; as an option, axles carrying an unsprung mass and a sprung mass on a linear suspension, the wheel remaining in contact with the deck.
  • For track-bridge interaction: plane model (axis of the track and vertical), one track represented by a single rail bar (both rails), decks as beams on two bearings, elastic-plastic longitudinal track-support connection per metre of track, rigid vertical connection. Rail expansion devices are not modelled.
  • SI units: forces in N, loads per unit length in N/m, speeds in m/s (in km/h for the centrifugal force, as in the standard).

13.2 Vertical load models#

13.2.1 LM71, SW/0, SW/2 and unloaded train#

The models of EN 1991-2 §6.3 are vehicles in the sense of chapter 12: point forces and uniform loads at fixed distances from a reference, slid along the influence line. Their effect is (12.1), multiplied by the classification factor α for LM71 and SW/0:

E=α[j=1∑4​Qvk​η(r+aj​)+qvk​∫tails​ηds],Qvk​=250 kN,qvk​=80 kN/m(13.1)
  • LM71: four 250 kN axles spaced 1.60 m apart and, on either side, a load of 80 kN/m starting 0.80 m from the outer axles. In keta, each tail has a finite length of 20 m;
  • SW/0: two loads of 133 kN/m over 15 m, 5.30 m apart;
  • SW/2: two loads of 150 kN/m over 25 m, 7.00 m apart, without the factor α;
  • unloaded train: 10 kN/m, to be placed on the sign zones of the line as a free distributed load;
  • HSLM-B: N forces of 170 kN at the uniform spacing d, the number and the spacing being read by the user from figures 6.12 and 6.13 of the standard for the span studied.

The factor α is checked within the interval [0.7, 1.5] and not against the eight values of EN 1991-2 §6.3.2(3). The rule of application of LM71 that places the distributed part on all the adverse zones, whatever their number, omitting the favourable axles, is not programmed: with 20 m tails, a structure whose adverse zones extend beyond them must be supplemented by a free distributed load of 80 kN/m, according to (12.2).

13.2.2 Dynamic factors#

The static effects of the load models are increased by the dynamic factor of EN 1991-2 §6.4.5.2, a function of the determinant length LΦ​ (in m) given by the user from table 6.2:

Φ2​=LΦ​​−0.21.44​+0.82,1≤Φ2​≤1.67Φ3​=LΦ​​−0.22.16​+0.73,1≤Φ3​≤2.0(13.2)

Φ2​ corresponds to carefully maintained track, Φ3​ to standard maintenance. For LΦ​ = 10 m, Φ2​ = 1.306 and Φ3​ = 1.459; for 4 m, Φ2​ = 1.62. A length below 0.04 m is refused.

13.2.3 Eccentricity of vertical loads#

EN 1991-2 §6.3.5 allows an unequal sharing of the loads between the two rails, in a ratio q2​/q1​ of at most 1.25. For a total load Q and a spacing r of the wheel loads (1.50 m by default),

q1​=1+q2​/q1​Q​,q2​=Q−q1​,e=q2​+q1​q2​−q1​​2r​=18r​ for q2​/q1​=1.25(13.3)

that is, an eccentricity of 0.083 m for r = 1.50 m. keta refuses a ratio outside [1, 1.25].

13.2.4 Distribution by the sleepers and the ballast#

For local effects, an axle load Q is shared over three consecutive sleepers, as Q/4, Q/2 and Q/4 (EN 1991-2 §6.3.6.1), then spread through the ballast at a slope of 4 to 1 (§6.3.6.2). Under a sleeper of width b and length ℓ, above a ballast depth h, the load is distributed at deck level over a rectangle

ax​=b+2h​(longitudinally),ay​=ℓ+2h​(transversely)(13.4)

For a twin-block sleeper, each block of transverse width bblock​ carries half the load under its rail, over ay​=bblock​+h/2. The default values are h = 0.30 m, b = 0.25 m, ℓ = 2.60 m, a block of 0.60 m, a rail spacing of 1.50 m and sleepers spaced a = 0.60 m apart. A zero depth represents slab track; the sharing over three sleepers may be suppressed. On an influence surface, the effect of the contact areas is the sum of the uniform pressures integrated over each rectangle:

E=i∑​ax​ay​Qi​​∬recti​​η(x,y)dxdy(13.5)

13.3 Horizontal forces#

13.3.1 Traction and braking#

The traction and braking forces of EN 1991-2 §6.5.3 are uniformly distributed over the influence length Lab​ (in m):

Qlak​=αmin(33Lab​, 1000) kN,Qlbk​=αmin(20Lab​, 6000) kN(LM71, SW/0, HSLM),Qlbk​=35Lab​ kN(SW/2)(13.6)

The cap applies before the multiplication by α, which the user sets to 1 for the models that are not classified. For Lab​ = 40 m, Qlak​ = 1000 kN and Qlbk​ = 800 kN.

13.3.2 Nosing and centrifugal force#

The nosing force (§6.5.2) is a force of 100 kN at rail level, multiplied by α only if α>1. The centrifugal force (§6.5.1) of a characteristic vertical load Qvk​ (or of a load per unit length qvk​) on a track of radius r (m) travelled at V (km/h) is

Qtk​=127rV2​fQvk​,f=1−1000V−120​(V814​+1.75)(1−Lf​2.88​​)(13.7)

where Lf​ is the influence length of the loaded part of the curve. f equals 1 for V≤ 120 km/h or Lf​≤ 2.88 m, and a speed above 300 km/h is taken as 300 km/h; a length Lf​ above 150 m is taken as 150 m. For V = 200 km/h and Lf​ = 20 m, f = 0.711. An infinite radius (straight track) gives a zero force. The height at which the force is applied and the combination with Φ are left to the user.

13.4.2 Speed sweep#

The train enters the running path (a sequence of bars) at time 0, head at the origin, and travels along it at constant speed v until its last axle has left it, followed by a free vibration time of the user's choice. The response is computed by modal superposition on the given modes, normalised with respect to the mass:

q¨​n​+2ζn​ωn​q˙​n​+ωn2​qn​=pn​(t)=j∑​Pj​φnT​g(vt+aj​)(13.8)

where g(s) is the vector of the nodal forces equivalent to a unit force placed at abscissa s of the path (zero off the path). For a force placed within a bar, these forces are the shape functions of the element (cubic in bending, linear in axial force): the modal force φnT​g is therefore a third-degree polynomial of the position, which keta reconstructs without error from four positions per bar (Lagrange interpolation at the third points). Each modal oscillator is advanced by the analytical solution of a damped oscillator under a force varying linearly over the step (Nigam and Jennings; Chopra, Dynamics of Structures, table 5.2.1): the time step introduces no integration error, it only samples the movement of the loads. It is

Δt=min(ωmax​nT​2π​, vΔsmax​​)(13.9)

with nT​ = 20 steps per period of the highest mode retained and a train movement of at most Δsmax​ = 0.5 m per step, rounded so that the total duration contains a whole number of steps. The modal acceleration is drawn from equation (13.8) at the end of each step.

13.4.3 Vehicle-bridge interaction#

As an option, each axle carries an unsprung mass mw​, rolling on the deck without loss of contact, and a sprung mass ms​ connected to the wheel by a suspension of stiffness k and damping c. The displacement of the deck in the direction of the loads being w(s,t)=∑n​φn​(s)qn​(t), the wheel located at s=vt+a follows the deck with the kinematics of a moving point, convective terms included:

yw​=φTq,y˙​w​=φTq˙​+vφ′Tq,y¨​w​=φTq¨​+2vφ′Tq˙​+v2φ′′Tq(13.11)

the derivatives of the mode shapes along the path being drawn from the same cubic per bar. The contact force transmitted to the deck is

F=(mw​+ms​)g+k(ys​−yw​)+c(y˙​s​−y˙​w​)−mw​y¨​w​(13.12)

and the sprung mass obeys ms​y¨​s​+c(y˙​s​−y˙​w​)+k(ys​−yw​)=0. The modes of the bridge and the sprung masses form a linear system with time-varying coefficients, integrated by the average-acceleration Newmark scheme (second order in Δt); the step also accounts for the natural frequency of the suspensions. The static envelope is that of the axle weights.

For a direct time integration on the finite element model (chapter 15), keta provides the history of the nodal forces of a vehicle: at each step, each axle present on the path is replaced by its equivalent nodal forces (shape functions of the element). The position of the reference of the vehicle is

13.5 Track-bridge interaction (UIC 774-3)#

13.5.1 Model#

The continuous welded rail, restrained by the ballast, resists the longitudinal movements of the deck: the temperature variations of the deck, its deflection under the train and the braking forces are shared between the rail, the embankments and the bearings. keta builds the plane model of UIC leaflet 774-3 (and of EN 1991-2 §6.5.4):

  • the track is a line of bars at rail level, from the origin to the end of the right-hand embankment, divided into elements of 2 m by default; its two ends are restrained longitudinally (the welded rail continues); the embankments measure 300 m on each side by default;
  • each deck is a line of bars at its neutral axis, at the distance et​ below the rail, connected by rigid arms to rail level and, at its two ends, to the level of the bearings, at ea​ below the neutral axis; each bearing is a longitudinal spring of given stiffness (zero for a sliding bearing) and rigid vertically;
  • at each node of the track, a longitudinal spring connects the rail to the ground (embankment) or to the top of the deck, with the track law per metre multiplied by the tributary length of the node; a node at a deck end shares its two half-lengths between the two supports. The vertical connection is rigid: the vertical load of the train passes into the deck, whose end rotation moves the top of the deck and the bearings longitudinally.

13.5.2 Track law#

Each interaction spring is elastic-plastic, of stiffness k and resistance fy​ (per metre of track, multiplied by the tributary length), with kinematic hardening of slope kp​ (zero here). With the plastic slip up​ and the back force β,

F=k(d−up​),∣F−β∣≤fy​(13.14)

The unloaded track and the loaded track each have their own stiffness and resistance, given by the user from UIC leaflet 774-3 or EN 1991-2 §6.5.4. Each load increment is solved by an implicit Euler scheme: for each spring, the incremental law is a monotonic piecewise linear function of the final deformation, so that the increment is a convex problem solved by a finite Newton method, then the slip and the back force are updated in closed form.

13.5.3 Course of the analysis#

The analysis follows the sequence of the leaflet. The temperature variation of the rail and that of each deck (uniform) are applied first, in ten increments by default, with the unloaded track law everywhere. Then, for each position of the train (from the initial head position to the final position, 16 positions by default), the analysis restarts from the thermal state, gives the loaded law to the springs located under the train, and applies the train (horizontal braking or traction load per metre, signed, and vertical load per metre, 20 kN/m and 80 kN/m by default over 300 m) in ten increments. The change of law preserves the force in the spring: the new law starts from the state reached,

up​←d−k′F​,β←0(13.15)

and a force greater than the new resistance is brought back to it, the out-of-balance being resolved by the next increment. The slip acquired under the thermal effect is thus preserved when the train arrives.

13.5.4 Results#

keta provides, for the thermal state, for each position of the train and as an envelope over the positions: the extreme stresses in the rail σ=N/A (compression negative), the longitudinal forces in the two bearings of each deck, and the maximum relative displacements track-deck and track-embankment. The criteria of the leaflet (permissible additional rail stresses, relative displacements) are not checked by keta.

Two continuous solutions make it possible to judge the model. A rail restrained along its whole length under a temperature variation ΔT has the stress σ=−EαΔT, that is −126 MPa for 50 K, a value keta recovers. A rail on an elastic support of stiffness k per metre, loaded longitudinally by q over a length 2a, obeys EAu′′−ku+q=0, and the maximum axial force, at the ends of the loaded zone, is

Nmax​=2λq​(1−e−2λa),λ=EAk​​(13.16)

For q = 20 kN/m over 100 m, k = 20 MN/m per metre and two rails of 76.9 cm², the stress is 8.26 MPa. Since the springs are lumped at the nodes, keta converges to this value at first order in the element length: 8.93 MPa with 2 m elements, 8.42 MPa with 0.5 m elements. Elements shorter than the default value are therefore useful when the rail stress governs.
