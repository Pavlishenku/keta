Chapter 6 Supports, links and soils

A structure stands on its supports and on the ground, and its parts are connected to one another by links: laminated elastomeric bearings, seismic stoppers, expansion joints, closure hinges, partial fixities, footings and piles. This chapter describes how keta represents these boundary conditions and devices: rigid, elastic or skew supports, supports that can only push or that cap their force, rigid links and linear relations between degrees of freedom, springs between nodes, linear or with a nonlinear law, and soil, whether distributed under a bar (Winkler foundation, zoned soil, pseudo-plastic soil) or lumped into springs (earth pressure, pile curves). For each device, we state which problem is solved, by which method, and which results are returned. The elimination of degrees of freedom and the computation of reactions are described in chapter 4; the combination of nonlinear results, in chapter 5.

6.1 Assumptions#

  • displacements are small: supports, links and springs act in their initial position, and their axes do not rotate with the structure;
  • a support or a link acts at a node; Winkler soil acts along a bar, at each point, in proportion to the local displacement of that point, with no interaction between neighbouring points;
  • the linear devices (rigid and elastic supports, rigid and elastic links, linear relations, elastic soil) keep the problem linear and superposable;
  • the nonlinear devices (unilateral supports, force caps, link laws, pseudo-plastic soil) are handled in statics, one load case at a time; their results are not superposable;
  • the nonlinear laws are piecewise linear; they are elastic (without memory), except for the staged analysis with loading history described further on;
  • friction, damping and the time dependence of the devices are not represented here (link damping enters dynamics, chapters 15 and 16).

6.2 Rigid supports and imposed displacements#

A rigid support restrains one or more of the six components of a node in the global axes. The restrained degrees of freedom s are eliminated from the system; an imposed displacement uˉs​ (settlement, support rotation) moves to the right-hand side:

Kff​uf​=Ff​−Kfs​uˉs​,Rs​=Ksf​uf​+Kss​uˉs​−Fs​(6.1)

The matrix does not depend on the imposed displacement: a settlement is a load case like any other, and it can be combined. Imposed displacements apply only to rigidly restrained components. The reaction Rs​ is the force that the support exerts on the structure; the reactions together balance the applied loads.

Some components are restrained for modelling purposes rather than by a device: the out-of-plane degrees of freedom of a plane model, for example. These restraints return no reaction, so that an envelope over the construction stages does not pick up the zero reaction of a deactivated bearing. A component that receives no stiffness is restrained automatically, and a warning is issued.

6.3 Elastic supports#

6.3.1 Stiffness per degree of freedom#

A support spring of stiffness k (N/m or N·m/rad) on component c of a node adds k to the corresponding diagonal term of the stiffness matrix. The component stays free, and the reaction returned is the force of the spring on the structure,

Kcc​←Kcc​+k,Rc​=−kuc​(6.2)

obtained from the equilibrium of the node, with no special treatment. A component that is both restrained and fitted with a spring is eliminated: the spring then has no effect and does not enter the reaction.

6.3.2 Coupled 6 × 6 matrix#

A general elastic support is a symmetric matrix Ks​ of dimension 6×6, expressed in support axes rotated by an angle α about Z. It is given by its lower triangle (21 terms; 6 terms for a plane model, on ux​,uy​,θz​) or by its diagonal alone. With R=diag(Rz​(α),Rz​(α)), which takes the components from the global axes to the support axes, the assembled matrix is

Kg​=RTKs​R(6.3)

A spring along the x axis of a support rotated by 90° thus becomes a spring along Y. A negative diagonal term is rejected. This matrix adds to the springs per degree of freedom and, like them, returns the reaction −Kg​u. It represents an elastomeric bearing (shear and compression stiffnesses), a short condensed pier, or a foundation whose static impedance matrix is known.

6.4 Skew supports#

A skew support restrains components in arbitrary axes, given by its three orthonormal axes ax​,ay​,az​: a bearing sliding along the axis of a curved bridge, an oblique guide. Each restrained local component is a linear relation on the translations (or the rotations) of the node,

ak​⋅u=0(6.4)

keta eliminates one global component for each of them: the relations of a given node are put in reduced row echelon form, taking as pivot the largest component still free, so that two restrained directions (a guide and a longitudinal stopper) do not produce a cyclic chain. The reaction is returned in the support axes and in the global axes:

Rglobal​=k restrained∑​μk​ak​(6.5)

where μk​ is the local component of the reaction. A component for which no free global component remains to be eliminated is rejected.

6.5 Unilateral supports and force caps#

6.5.1 Contact conditions#

A bearing sitting on a pier can push the deck upwards but cannot hold it down. For each unilateral component, the Kuhn-Tucker conditions are imposed, with σ=+1 if the admissible reaction is positive and −1 if it is negative:

σR≥0,σu≥0,Ru=0(6.6)

The support is either active (u=0, reaction of the right sign) or lifted off (R=0, displacement moving away from the support). A variant acts as a block: the criterion bears on a global axis, and when it is crossed, all the components of the support are released together (a support that lifts off no longer holds either the horizontal forces or the moments). The two variants cannot be combined on the same support, and a unilateral component cannot be combined with a 6×6 support matrix (block lift-off, for its part, allows it).

6.5.2 Force cap#

A restrained component may be given a cap Rmax​: the support stays restrained as long as ∣R∣≤Rmax​; beyond that, it yields and transmits exactly

R=±Rmax​as long as the node penetrates the support(6.7)

with the sign of the reaction that made it yield. The behaviour is rigid-plastic. On a unilateral component, the cap acts on the admissible side. For a continuous beam of two 10 m spans, only one of which carries 10 kN/m, the reaction at the intermediate pier is 10qL/16=62.5 kN for an Euler-Bernoulli beam (slightly less with shear deformation); capped at 60 kN, it is 60 kN, and the equilibrium of the remaining beam gives 45 kN at the loaded support and −5 kN at the other end: this is what keta returns.

6.5.3 Active-set method#

keta solves these conditions by an active-set method. It starts with all supports active; at each iteration, the structure is solved with the current set, the lifted-off supports being removed and the yielded supports replaced by their force ±Rmax​. Each component is then examined, and it changes state according to the tests

​active→lifted offactive→yieldedlifted off→activeyielded→active​​if σR<−10−9(1+∣R∣)if ∣R∣>Rmax​(1+10−12)if σu<−10−12 m (the node passes through the support)if su>10−12 m (the node no longer penetrates)​(6.8)

where s is the sign of the reaction that made the support yield. The analysis stops when no component changes state. The matrix is refactorised at each iteration. The number of iterations is limited to 18na​+10, na​ being the number of supports; beyond that, the analysis stops with an error. A support whose components are all released stays in the list of reactions, with a zero reaction.

The result depends on the load: each case is solved on its own, its results are flagged as not superposable, and the model's combinations are rejected (chapter 5). A combination must be described as a case, with all its loads.

6.6 Kinematic links#

6.6.1 Rigid links#

A rigid link connects one or more slave nodes to a master node as if they belonged to the same rigid body: a pier head, a very stiff diaphragm, the connection of a deck to its axis. For the coupled components (a mask in global axes), the displacement of the slave is

us​=um​+θm​×(xs​−xm​),θs​=θm​(6.9)

The slave degrees of freedom are eliminated without approximation, u=Tur​, and the system solved is TTKTur​=TTF (chapter 4). Chains (the slave of one link being the master of another) are resolved; a cyclic chain, a component constrained twice and a supported slave are rejected: the master is supported instead, or the support is connected through an elastic link. By releasing the three rotations of the mask, a spherical hinge of zero length is obtained; since the mask is in global axes, a single bending rotation in a local frame cannot be released in this way.

6.6.2 Linear relations#

A general linear relation ties components of arbitrary nodes:

k∑​ck​u(nk​,jk​)=0(6.10)

The component of the first term is eliminated, u1​=−∑k>1​(ck​/c1​)uk​; it must be free, carry no other relation and have a non-zero coefficient. These relations join the same reduction as the rigid links. keta returns for each of them its multiplier μ: the force it exerts on the structure is −μck​ on each of its degrees of freedom.

6.7 Elastic links#

6.7.1 Spring between two nodes#

An elastic link is a symmetric 6×6 spring k located at the point P=xi​+s(xj​−xi​), which the two nodes reach through rigid arms (s=0.5 by default). The relative displacement at point P is

d=(uj​+θj​×(P−xj​))−(ui​+θi​×(P−xi​)),Δθ=θj​−θi​(6.11)

so that a rigid-body motion of the pair does not deform the spring. Expressed in the axes of the link, (d,Δθ)=Bue​, it gives the element matrix

Ke​=BTkB(6.12)

The force on node j is −k(d,Δθ); the axial component is positive in tension when the x axis runs from i to j. The axes of the link are either given explicitly or taken as those of a bar from i to j (with its roll angle); for two coincident nodes, they are the global axes. A link between two coincident nodes is an elastic hinge: partial fixity, semi-rigid closure hinge, bearing between deck and pier. The results of a link are its deformation and its force, in its own axes.

6.7.2 Nonlinear link laws#

Each component of a link may follow a nonlinear law F(d) in place of its stiffness term (it must then have no coupling term with the other components). The law is piecewise linear, defined by points (di​,Fi​) with strictly increasing abscissae and non-decreasing forces; beyond the end points, the end segments are extended. Four laws are predefined: tension only, compression only, a gap g on either side before the stiffness k acts, and elastic-perfectly plastic (k up to ∣F∣=Fy​, then a slope kp​≥0). The force at zero displacement may be non-zero: a preloaded spring, earth pressure at rest.

A non-decreasing law derives from a convex potential Ψj​(d)=∫0d​Fj​. Equilibrium is then the minimum of the potential energy

Π(u)=21​uTKu−fTu+j∑​Ψj​(bjT​u+d0,j​)(6.13)

where K is the stiffness of the linear part (rigid links eliminated), bj​ the operator that gives the deformation of component j, and d0,j​ the part of that deformation due to imposed displacements. keta minimises Π by Newton's method, with the tangent matrix K+∑j​kt,j​bj​bjT​ (kt,j​ being the slope of the current segment), and a line search that halves the step as long as the energy does not decrease. Since the potential is piecewise quadratic, the full step gives the solution as soon as every law is on its final segment: the analysis ends in a finite number of iterations, with no tolerance on the laws and no penalty. It stops when the full step is accepted and the norm of the residual is below 10−11 times the largest load (or the round-off error of Ku), within a limit of 200 iterations. A decreasing law is rejected, and a mechanism (a node held only by the slack component of a link) stops the analysis.

For a simply supported 20 m beam, of midspan stiffness kb​=48EI/L3, resting at midspan on a link of stiffness k with gap g, under a force P, equilibrium is w=−(P+kg)/(kb​+k) once the gap has closed: keta finds it in two iterations, as it does for the tension-only, compression-only and elastic-plastic cases.

6.7.3 Softening laws and moment-rotation laws depending on axial force#

A hinge whose moment decreases after the peak (the ASCE 41 backbone curves, for example) no longer derives from a convex potential. keta then follows the equilibrium path under control of a displacement uc​: the unknowns are (u,λ) and the bordered system

r=fperm​+λf−Ku−j∑​bj​Fj​(bjT​u)=0,uc​=uctarget​(6.14)

is solved by Newton's method with the exact tangent, in 20 steps per branch of the imposed path, at most 50 iterations and a tolerance of 10−10, the step being halved in case of failure (at most ten times). Permanent loads may be applied first, at λ=0. To get past a reversal of the controlled displacement, the path can be followed by arc length, from event to event (a change of segment in a law), each point of the path being an equilibrium state. A rotation component may follow a family of laws M(θ) tabulated for several axial forces N0​<N1​<… of the same link: the law at N is the linear interpolation, point by point, of the two laws that bracket it, and the tangent includes the term ∂M/∂N; an axial force outside the table stops the analysis.

6.7.4 Elastic-plastic loading history#

The longitudinal resistance of ballast, a friction bearing or a fuse retain a plastic slip from one loading step to the next. For these components, keta uses an elastic-plastic law with linear kinematic hardening: elastic stiffness k, threshold Fy​, post-yield slope kp​ (0≤kp​<k), plastic slip up​ and back force β:

F=k(d−up​),∣F−β∣≤Fy​,H=k−kp​kkp​​(6.15)

A step adds the loads of a case in equal increments (ten by default). Over an increment, the return mapping (implicit Euler) turns each component into a piecewise linear law of its final deformation, of slope k between (d−,β−Fy​) and (d+,β+Fy​), d±=up​+(β±Fy​)/k, and of slope kp​ beyond: the increment is the convex problem (6.13), solved as above, after which up​ and β are updated. Between two steps, the law of a component may change (ballast unloaded, then loaded under the train): the force is preserved and the new law starts from that force; a force above the new threshold is brought back to it, and the out-of-balance is absorbed by the next step. A committed state may serve as the starting point of several loading sequences (each position of a train from the same thermal state, chapter 13).

6.8 Soils#

6.8.1 Winkler elastic foundation#

A Winkler soil exerts on the bar, at each point, a reaction proportional to the local displacement of that point. The moduli are given in the local axes of the bar, per unit length: kfx​,kfy​,kfz​ in N/m per m (Pa) and kmx​ in N·m/rad per m. With an Euler-Bernoulli bar, the equations solved are

EIw′′′′+kw=q(x),EAu′′−kfx​u=−px​(x),GJθ′′−kmx​θ=−mx​(x)(6.16)

the first one for each bending plane. The characteristic length ℓ=1/β, with β=(k/4EI)1/4, measures the reach of the effect of a concentrated load: under a force P at the middle of an infinite beam,

w(x)=2kPβ​e−β∣x∣(cosβx+sinβ∣x∣),M(x)=4βP​e−β∣x∣(cosβx−sinβ∣x∣)(6.17)

The soil reaction is returned at the study sections, p=kulocal​: it has the sign of the local displacement, and the force of the soil on the bar is −p.

6.8.2 Exact element for a bar on soil#

In linear statics, a bar on uniform soil without release, without shear deformation and of constant cross-section is handled by an exact element. Without shear deformation means that the shear areas of the section are zero: a parametric section, which carries them by default (chapter 3), keeps the consistent bed described below unless they are set to zero. The exact element is built on the solutions of (6.16) themselves. Following the note by A. L. Millan (Annales de l'ITBTP no. 408), keta uses the transfer functions

fp​(x)=n≥0∑​(−EIk​)n(p+4n)!xp+4n​,gp​(x)=n≥0∑​(EAk​)n(p+2n)!xp+2n​(6.18)

(fp′​=fp−1​) in bending and in tension or torsion; their power series remains valid as k→0, with no discontinuity. The functions f4​,f5​ (g2​,g3​) give the particular solution for linearly distributed loads; concentrated loads and imposed strains (thermal expansion, curvature) are also taken into account. A transfer matrix over a long, strongly supported bar grows as eβL and becomes ill-conditioned. keta therefore divides the bar into pieces of length l≤(EI/k)1/4 (l≤EA/k​ in tension and torsion), builds the exact stiffness and the fixed-end forces of each piece, then condenses the interior nodes: the result remains the exact solution of the equation, with no discretisation error, whatever βL.

A single bar is enough, and the internal forces at the study sections include the soil reaction. For a 66 m beam (βL=20) loaded at its middle and represented by one bar, the deflection and the moment under the load differ from (6.17) by 6×10−9 in relative terms, a difference that is due to the finite length.

6.8.3 Consistent Hermite bed#

When the exact element does not apply (Timoshenko bar, release, variable section), and in the dynamic, stability, nonlinear and influence-line analyses, the soil stiffness is the consistent matrix

ksoil​=∫0L​NTkNdx(6.19)

built on the shape functions of the element: cubic Hermite functions in bending, linear functions in tension and torsion. It is added after the condensation of releases (a hinge releases the moment of the bar, not the soil reaction). At the nodes, the solution converges to that of (6.16) as the fourth power of the bar length; inside a bar, the internal forces returned do not include the soil reaction. 6.8.4 Zoned soil#

The modulus may vary along a bar by zones: each zone carries its moduli kfx​,kfy​,kfz​,kmx​ and the abscissa xz​ of its end; the last zone extends to the end of the bar, whatever abscissa is given. The consistent matrix of the bar is the sum of the integrals (6.19) over each zone,

ksoil​=z∑​∫xz−1​xz​​NTdiag(kfx​,kfy​,kfz​,kmx​)z​Ndx,x0​=0(6.20)

computed by an 8-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature, exact for these polynomials of degree 6 at most. When the exact element applies to every bar on soil, each zone becomes a zone of the exact element.

6.8.5 Pseudo-plastic soil and tensionless soil#

Real soil does not pull, and its reaction is bounded. For each bar and each component, a threshold ff​ (a force per unit length, N/m, or a moment per unit length) and a unilaterality may be given: the soil reacts only where p=ku is positive, or only where it is negative. The reaction becomes

r(x)={0clamp(ku(x), −ff​, ff​)​if p(x) does not have the admissible signotherwise​(6.21)

The threshold is an intensity per unit length: a limit pressure must be multiplied by the width of the footing or the diameter of the pile, as the modulus is. keta solves this problem by an active set on sub-segments: each zone is divided into ten sub-segments (an adjustable number), and the state of each sub-segment and each component is decided by the mean reaction over the sub-segment. When elastic, the sub-segment keeps its modulus; when capped, it loses its modulus and receives a constant distributed load of intensity ff​ opposing the motion; when lifted off, it carries nothing. The structure is solved again until no state changes any more, in at most 50 iterations. A capped or lifted-off sub-segment becomes elastic again only once it has clearly returned to the admissible domain (a band of 10−8 in relative terms), which prevents oscillation between two states. The yield front is located to within one sub-segment.

By default, each bar on soil is the exact element with the moduli of its states (zero where the soil is capped or lifted off), and the mean reaction of a sub-segment is the integral of the exact deflected shape. Unlike linear statics, this analysis uses the exact element even when the section carries shear areas: shear deformation is then neglected. If a bar on soil carries a release or a variable section, the whole model uses the consistent matrix (6.19); the bars must then be finely divided. For the previous beam, divided into eight bars and capped at 100 kN/m, the soil reaction is 100 kN/m under the load and its resultant equals the applied force to within round-off.

The results of a unilateral or capped soil are not superposable; combinations are formed only if no soil in the model is nonlinear. Zoned and pseudo-plastic soils are also available in the staged analysis (chapter 10).

6.8.6 Nonlinear soil springs#

The soil around a retaining wall or a pile can also be represented by springs lumped at the nodes: a zero-length link between the node and a fixed node created at its position, whose axis is the direction of action and whose law is one of the nonlinear laws above. They are solved by the minimisation (6.13).

For earth pressure on a wall, each soil layer gives the effective vertical stress σv​(y)=σv,0​+γ(ytop​−y) and, from the coefficients Ka​,K0​,Kp​ and the cohesion,

p0​=K0​σv​,pa​=max(0, Ka​σv​−Kac​c),pp​=Kp​σv​+Kpc​c,p(d)=clamp(p0​−kh​d, pa​, pp​)(6.22)

where d is the displacement of the wall towards the excavation (the active direction) and kh​=A+B(ytop​−y)κ the modulus of subgrade reaction. The pressures are distributed to the nodes of the wall consistently: each characteristic level of a node is the integral of the field multiplied by the linear shape function of the node, over the part of its two segments lying within the layer, then multiplied by the width. The sum of the plateau forces is then the exact thrust, Ka​(qH+γH2/2) for a homogeneous layer. A lumped distribution (the field at the level of the node multiplied by its tributary length) is also offered. The usual coefficients are provided: Rankine, Ka​=tan2(π/4−φ/2) and Kp​=tan2(π/4+φ/2); Jaky, K0​=1−sinφ; Coulomb for a vertical wall and a horizontal backfill, with the wall friction δ,

Ka​=cosδ(1+cosδsin(φ+δ)sinφ​​)2cos2φ​(6.23)

and the same expression with a minus sign inside the square for Kp​.

For piles, keta builds the API RP 2A curves (21st edition, §6.7 and 6.8), per unit length of pile (to be multiplied by the tributary length of the node): p-y curves for soft clays (Matlock), stiff clays above the water table (Welch and Reese) and sands (p=Apu​tanh(kXy/Apu​)), t-z curves for shaft friction and q-z curves for end bearing (compression only). Curves defined by a formula are sampled on the curve and joined by chords (16 segments by default). The post-peak loss of friction of the t-z curves is not represented: a decreasing law would break the convexity of the problem, and the peak is retained.

6.8.7 Edge springs#

A shell edge resting on an elastic support, or a semi-rigid joint between two slabs, is a line of springs of stiffness k per unit length (a 6×6 matrix in the axes of each segment: x along the segment, z upwards). keta represents it by node-to-node elastic links; each node receives half of each of its two segments,

Knode​=adjacent segments∑​2Ls​​RsT​kRs​(6.24)

which is exact for a relative displacement that is uniform along the line, and a discretisation of the line of springs otherwise. Without an opposite line, each node is connected to a fixed node created at its position. A hinge line is obtained by doubling the nodes, coupling their translations by rigid links and adding, if needed, a rotational spring.

6.9 Scope#

All the devices of this chapter act under small displacements and in their initial axes. Unilateral supports, force caps and link laws are static analyses case by case, not superposable; the linear analyses (eigenmodes, buckling, influence lines) reject links with a nonlinear law and treat a unilateral support as bilateral. Imposed displacements apply only to rigidly restrained components. The plastic slip of a support is carried from one case to the next only by elastic-plastic links loaded in steps, not by the supports of the model. The exact element for a bar on soil is restricted to the linear statics of prismatic Euler-Bernoulli bars without release; everywhere else, the consistent matrix requires a division with βh≤0.5. The pseudo-plastic soil locates its front to within one sub-segment and decides the state of a sub-segment from its mean reaction.
