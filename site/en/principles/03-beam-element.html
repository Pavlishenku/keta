Chapter 3 Beam element

The bar is the basic element of a framed structure: deck beam, pier, cross-girder, truss chord or diagonal. This chapter describes what keta knows about an isolated bar: its kinematics, the relation between the displacements of its ends and the forces it exerts there (its stiffness matrix), the way its modelling features (offsets, hinges, variable cross-section) modify this relation, and the way the loads applied between its nodes are passed to the global system as equivalent nodal forces. The assembly of these contributions and the computation of the internal forces between the nodes are the subject of chapter 4; the geometric stiffness, which adds the effect of the axial force on bending, that of chapter 17; non-uniform torsion, that of chapter 19. The conventions for axes and signs are those of chapter 2.

3.1 Assumptions#

  • the bar is straight between its two ends; its cross-section is constant, or varies according to one of the laws of the section “Bars with variable cross-section”;
  • the material is linear elastic, with modulus E and shear modulus G, equal to E/2(1+ν) for an isotropic material or given directly (timber, orthotropic materials);
  • cross-sections remain plane. In Timoshenko theory, adopted by default, they may rotate independently of the slope of the centre line, which introduces shear deformation; with a zero shear area, they remain normal to the centre line (Euler-Bernoulli theory);
  • torsion is uniform (Saint-Venant theory) and uncoupled from bending: the shear centre coincides with the centroid;
  • displacements are small and equilibrium is written on the initial geometry; the axial force has no effect on bending (see chapter 17 for second order);
  • the y, z axes are the principal axes of the cross-section: the two bending problems are uncoupled.

3.2 Kinematics and constitutive laws#

3.2.1 Displacement field#

A cross-section at abscissa x is defined by the translation (u,v,w) of its centroid and by its rotation (θx​,θy​,θz​). Since cross-sections remain plane, the displacement of a point (y,z) of the cross-section is

u(x,y,z)=u(x)+zθy​(x)−yθz​(x),v(x,y,z)=v(x)−zθx​(x),w(x,y,z)=w(x)+yθx​(x)(3.1)

This gives the axial strain ε=u′+zθy′​−yθz′​ (relation (2.12) of chapter 2) and the mean shear strains of the cross-section, which are the difference between the slope of the centre line and the rotation of the cross-section:

γxy​=v′−θz​,γxz​=w′+θy​(3.2)

The internal forces, defined in chapter 2, are related to these strains by the constitutive laws of the cross-section:

N=EAu′,Vy​=GAvy​γxy​,Vz​=GAvz​γxz​,T=GJθx′​,My​=EIy​θy′​,Mz​=EIz​θz′​(3.3)

where Avy​ and Avz​ are the shear areas along y and z and J the Saint-Venant torsion constant. The equilibrium of an elementary segment, under distributed loads (px​,qy​,qz​) and distributed moments (mx​,my​,mz​) per unit length, reads, with the vector convention of chapter 2,

N′=−px​,Vy′​=−qy​,Vz′​=−qz​,T′=−mx​,My′​=Vz​−my​,Mz′​=−Vy​−mz​(3.4)

Eliminating the internal forces gives, for Euler-Bernoulli theory (γ=0, hence θy​=−w′ and θz​=v′), the classical equations EIy​w′′′′=qz​ and EIz​v′′′′=qy​.

3.2.2 Shear areas#

The parameter that measures the importance of shear deformation is, for each bending plane,

Φy​=GAvy​L212EIz​​,Φz​=GAvz​L212EIy​​(3.5)

Φy​ applies to bending in the xy plane (deflection v) and Φz​ to bending in the xz plane (deflection w). It is of the order of (h/L)2: negligible for a slender beam, it reaches a few tenths for a deep beam, a crosshead or a short pier segment. A zero shear area means a cross-section infinitely stiff in shear: Φ=0 and the Euler-Bernoulli beam is recovered exactly.

For parametric cross-sections (rectangle, circle, tube, I-section, box, tee) and those of the catalogue, the shear area is by default the one that gives the same strain energy as the actual distribution of shear stresses in simple bending, solution of the Saint-Venant flexure problem:

2GAv​V2​=∫A​2Gτxy2​+τxz2​​dA(3.6)

It depends on Poisson's ratio of the material of the bar and on the proportions of the cross-section; a cross-section shared by bars of different materials receives the areas that suit each of them. For the solid circle, the closed form is Av​/A=6(1+ν)2/(7+14ν+8ν2), i.e. 0.851 for ν=0.3. For a 0.8 × 0.4 m rectangle and ν=0.2, the area along the smaller dimension is 0.807 times the area of the cross-section, instead of the shape factor 5/6; along the larger dimension, 0.833. I-sections and boxes are handled by the numerical analysis of the cross-section described in chapter 20. The classical shape factors (5/6 for the rectangle, 0.9 for the circle, the web area for an I-section) may be chosen instead, and a shear area given explicitly for a cross-section defined by its properties is always kept.

3.3 Stiffness matrix#

3.3.1 The four uncoupled problems#

In the local axes, and under the above assumptions, the twelve degrees of freedom of the bar split into four independent problems: tension-compression (ui​,uj​), torsion (θxi​,θxj​), bending in the xy plane (vi​,θzi​,vj​,θzj​) and bending in the xz plane (wi​,θyi​,wj​,θyj​).

In tension and in torsion, the stiffness is that of a spring:

kN​=LEA​[1−1​−11​],kT​=LGJ​[1−1​−11​](3.7)

In bending in the xy plane, for the unknowns (vi​,θzi​,vj​,θzj​),

kxy​=(1+Φy​)L3EIz​​​126L−126L​6L(4+Φy​)L2−6L(2−Φy​)L2​−12−6L12−6L​6L(2−Φy​)L2−6L(4+Φy​)L2​​(3.8)

and in the xz plane, for (wi​,θyi​,wj​,θyj​), the same matrix with Iy​ and Φz​, the coupling terms between deflection and rotation changing sign because θy​=−dw/dx:

kxz​=(1+Φz​)L3EIy​​​12−6L−12−6L​−6L(4+Φz​)L26L(2−Φz​)L2​−126L126L​−6L(2−Φz​)L26L(4+Φz​)L2​​(3.9)

These matrices are not an approximation: they are the exact relations between the end displacements and end forces of an unloaded prismatic Timoshenko beam, as obtained by the force method by inverting the flexibility of the cantilever. The presence of Φ reduces the relative translational stiffness from 12EI/L3 to 12EI/(1+Φ)L3, and the rotation terms become (4+Φ)EI/(1+Φ)L for the direct term and (2−Φ)EI/(1+Φ)L for the carry-over term. With Φ=0, the classical terms 12EI/L3, 6EI/L2, 4EI/L and 2EI/L are recovered.

3.3.2 Transformation to global axes#

The twelve-row local matrix k is made of these four blocks placed in the order of the degrees of freedom of chapter 2. In global axes, at the nodes,

Ke​=TTkT,T=diag(R,R,R,R)Tecc​(3.10)

where R is the rotation matrix of chapter 2 and Tecc​ the matrix of the rigid arms described below (the identity for a bar without offset). A hinge is treated before this change of axes, by condensation of k.

3.3.3 Saint-Venant torsion#

Torsion is uniform: the cross-section rotates about the x axis with its warping unrestrained, and the torsional moment is proportional to the rate of twist. For a distributed torque mx​, equilibrium and the constitutive law give

T=GJdxdθx​​,dxdT​=−mx​,f40​=∫0L​mx​(1−ξ)dx,f100​=∫0L​mx​ξdx(3.11)

the distribution of the torque between the two nodes being exact, since the twist of an unloaded bar varies linearly. The constant J is that of the cross-section (chapter 20): it equals Iy​+Iz​ for a circle or a tube, and much less for an open section, whose actual torsional stiffness depends on restrained warping. For an open section whose torsion matters (curved beam, lateral-torsional buckling), the model with seven degrees of freedom per node of chapter 19 takes over. A distributed torque mx​ and an imposed rate of twist κx​ are treated like their axial counterparts (see the loads below).

3.3.4 Truss bars#

A truss bar has stiffness only in tension-compression: its local matrix reduces to kN​, applied to the axial translations. The rotations of a node that carries only truss bars receive no stiffness; they are eliminated automatically (chapter 4). A transverse load applied to a truss bar is passed to the two nodes as by a simply supported beam,

fi0​=∫0L​q(1−ξ)dx,fj0​=∫0L​qξdx,ξ=Lx​(3.12)

and the internal forces returned at the output sections are those of this statically determinate beam (moment qL2/8 at midspan under a uniform load); the bar's own deflection under this load is not computed, the displacements between the nodes being interpolated linearly. A truss bar carries neither offset nor elastic foundation: these combinations are refused.

3.4 Offsets#

An offset shifts each end of the element from its node by a vector ei​ or ej​ given in global axes: edge beam whose centroid lies below the line of the nodes of a grillage, flange of a composite section, pier attached below a deck. The node and the end of the element are connected by an infinitely stiff arm, so that, from relation (2.3),

ui′​=ui​+θi​×ei​,[ui′​θi′​​]=[I0​−S(ei​)I​][ui​θi​​](3.13)

where S(e) is the skew-symmetric matrix such that S(e)θ=e×θ. The matrix Tecc​ combines these two transformations; it applies to the stiffness (3.10), to the equivalent nodal forces, to the mass and to the geometric stiffness. The element itself runs from i′=xi​+ei​ to j′=xj​+ej​: its length, its local axes and the abscissae of span loads refer to this segment, and not to the one joining the nodes.

An axial force in an offset bar thus produces a moment Ne at the node: a 6 m cantilever whose element is shifted 0.5 m above the line of the nodes, pulled by a 100 kN force applied at the end node, carries a constant moment My​=−50 kN·m, and the end node rotates by NeL/EIy​.

3.5 Releases#

Each of the twelve end components can be released: bending hinge, torsional release, sliding along the axis of the bar. A released component transmits no force; it is eliminated from the matrix of the bar by static condensation. Partitioning the local degrees of freedom into retained c and released r,

k~=kcc​−kcr​krr−1​krc​,f~0=fc0​−kcr​krr−1​fr0​(3.14)

Condensation introduces no approximation: the condensed bar has the stiffness and the internal forces of a bar fitted with perfect hinges. After the solution, the released displacements are recomputed from the retained displacements and the loads on the bar,

ur​=krr−1​(fr0​−krc​uc​)(3.15)

which gives, for example, the rotation of the pinned end of a beam fixed at the other end (qL3/48EI under a uniform load) and serves to compute the deflected shape between the nodes. If the releases leave the bar with a rigid-body motion (a bar released in torsion at both ends, for instance), krr​ is singular and the calculation is refused. Hinges at both ends of a bar whose torsion is retained, on the other hand, leave a stable bar, but may create a mechanism at the node: chapter 4 describes how it is detected. The geometric stiffness of a released bar is condensed with the same elastic operator.

3.6 Bars with variable cross-section#

3.6.1 Variation laws#

A bar may have a cross-section that varies along its axis: beam of variable depth, haunched crosshead, pier with a tapered shaft. keta knows three laws, each defined by the properties C (area, shear areas, second moments of area, torsion constant) at the ends, and at midlength for the parabolic law; t=x/L.

  • linear law along y and linear law along z: the dimension of the equivalent rectangle measured along that axis varies linearly, and each property varies as the power of this dimension that it contains, with p=1 for the areas and for the second moment about the other axis, p=3 for the second moment of area for bending in the plane of the varying dimension, and p=1 for the torsion constant, for want of an exponent that suits every cross-section;
  • parabolic law: the inverse of each property varies along the parabola through its values at the ends and at midlength; a parabola that vanishes within the bar is refused.
C(t)=[(1−t)Ci1/p​+tCj1/p​]p(linear law),C(t)1​=Ci​(1−t)(1−2t)​+Cm​4t(1−t)​+Cj​t(2t−1)​(parabolic law)(3.16)

A property that is zero at one end and non-zero at the other is refused; shear areas that are zero everywhere remove shear deformation, as for a prismatic bar.

3.6.2 Exact stiffness through the flexibility#

There is no closed form for the stiffness of a bar with variable cross-section, but the flexibility of the cantilever fixed at i and free at j is an integral of the internal forces due to the six unit actions applied at j:

Fjj​=∫0L​UT(x)D(x)U(x)dx,D=diag(EA1​,GAvy​1​,GAvz​1​,GJ1​,EIy​1​,EIz​1​)(3.17)

where column k of U(x) contains the six internal forces at section x due to unit action k; only the moments involve the lever arm, my​=My​−(L−x)Fz​ and mz​=Mz​+(L−x)Fy​. The stiffness of the free end is Kjj​=Fjj−1​, and the twelve-row matrix follows from the equilibrium of the bar, k=BTKjj​B, B expressing the transfer of forces from j to i. For a constant cross-section, the prismatic matrix (3.8) is recovered, shear included. The integral is computed by a 16-point Gauss-Legendre quadrature. It is exact for the parabolic law, whose integrands are polynomials of degree at most 4; for the linear laws, the integrands are rational functions with no pole on the bar and the quadrature converges geometrically: the error on the stiffness is of the order of 10−16 for a depth that doubles along the bar, and 10−7 for a ratio of ten. The tip deflection of an 8 m cantilever whose depth goes from 1.2 to 0.6 m, under a tip force, is thus obtained to machine precision.

The equivalent nodal forces of span loads (uniform, linear, point, thermal loads and uniform or linear imposed strains, self-weight ρA(x)g) are obtained on the same cantilever by the unit-load theorem, the integral being split at each discontinuity of the load. Point dislocations given in local axes are accepted; parabolic loads and parabolic imposed strains are refused on these bars.

A bar with variable cross-section cannot carry a release. Its mass and its geometric stiffness remain those of the constant cross-section assigned to the bar: modal and buckling analyses treat it with an average cross-section, not exactly. Its internal forces at the output sections are exact, and its displacements between the nodes are integrated with the local cross-section (chapter 4).

3.7 Bar loads and equivalent nodal forces#

3.7.1 Principle#

A load applied between the nodes is replaced, for the solution, by the nodal forces that do the same work in any displacement of the ends. If N(x) denotes the shape functions that give the displacement of the unloaded bar in terms of its twelve end displacements, the equivalent nodal forces are, in local axes,

f0=∫0L​NT(x)q(x)dx+k∑​NT(ak​)Pk​(3.18)

and the end forces of the bar are f=ku−f0: −f0 is the set of fixed-end forces of the bar under its load. When N is made of the exact solutions of the unloaded beam, which is the case here, the reciprocal theorem shows that (3.18) gives the exact fixed-end forces: the computed nodal displacements are exact whatever the load.

For the Timoshenko beam, the bending shape functions depend on Φ. In the xy plane, with ξ=x/L, v(x)=H1​vi​+H2​θzi​+H3​vj​+H4​θzj​ and

H1​H3​​=1+Φ1−3ξ2+2ξ3+Φ(1−ξ)​,=1+Φ3ξ2−2ξ3+Φξ​,​H2​H4​​=L1+Φξ−2ξ2+ξ3+2Φ​(ξ−ξ2)​,=L1+Φ−ξ2+ξ3−2Φ​(ξ−ξ2)​​(3.19)

In the xz plane, w=H1​wi​−H2​θyi​+H3​wj​−H4​θyj​. The work of a moment is done in the rotation of the cross-section, and not in the slope of the centre line, from which it differs by the shear strain; for moments, keta uses the shape functions of the rotation,

θz​(x)=(1+Φ)L6(ξ2−ξ)​(vi​−vj​)+1+Φ1−4ξ+3ξ2+Φ(1−ξ)​θzi​+1+Φ3ξ2−2ξ+Φξ​θzj​(3.20)

The axial and torsional displacements are interpolated linearly between the ends, which is exact for an unloaded bar. The integrals (3.18) are computed by a five-point Gauss quadrature over the loaded part of the bar, exact up to degree 9: it integrates without error the product of the cubic shape functions by a parabolic load.

The equivalent nodal forces serve only to obtain the displacements of the nodes. The internal forces between the nodes are then computed from the actual load (chapter 4).

3.7.2 Load axes#

A distributed or point load is given by its components in one of the following three sets of axes:

  • local axes of the bar: the components are taken as they are;
  • global axes: the load is given per metre of bar and brought into local axes by qℓ​=Rqg​;
  • projected axes: each global component is given per metre of projection of the bar on the plane perpendicular to that component, which is the natural way of describing snow or an imposed load on an inclined member.

For projected axes, component c of the intensity is multiplied by the ratio of the projected length to the length of the bar:

qcbar​=qc​1−ex,c2​​,c∈{X,Y,Z}(3.21)

where ex,c​ is component c of the unit vector of the bar. For a vertical load on a bar inclined at α to the horizontal, this ratio is cosα and the total load is qℓ, ℓ being the horizontal span; in global axes, it would be qL, with L the length of the bar.

3.7.3 Distributed loads#

A distributed load acts on all or part of the bar, over the interval [a,b] measured from the start of the element. Its intensity, a force vector per unit length, is uniform, linear between qa​ and qb​, or parabolic, defined by its values at the ends and at the middle of the interval, qm​:

q(s)=qa​+(qb​−qa​)t+4t(1−t)(qm​−2qa​+qb​​),t=b−as−a​(3.22)

Uniform and linear loads may also carry a distributed moment m(s), in N·m/m, which bends or twists the bar without shear force (for example the effect of an eccentric bonded tendon). For a bar fixed at both ends under a uniform load over its whole length, (3.18) gives qL/2 and qL2/12 at each end, whatever Φ. For a triangular load from 0 at i to q at j, the fixed-end moments depend on shear deformation:

Mi​=qL21+Φ301​+24Φ​​,Mj​=qL21+Φ201​+24Φ​​(3.23)

which gives back qL2/30 and qL2/20 for Φ=0 and brings both moments closer to qL2/24 as shear dominates. The following figure shows the equivalent nodal forces of a partial trapezoidal load, computed by keta for an Euler-Bernoulli bar; their sum is indeed the total load, 80 kN.

3.7.4 Point loads#

A force P and a moment C applied at abscissa a give f0=NT(a)P for the force, and the analogous expression with the rotation shape functions (3.20) for the moment. For a transverse force P on a bar fixed at both ends, with b=L−a, the fixed-end moment at i is

Mi​=L2Pab​1+Φb+ΦL/2​(3.24)

which gives back Pab2/L2 for Φ=0. A point load placed exactly on an end is transferred entirely to the corresponding node.

3.7.5 Self-weight#

Self-weight is a uniform load, given in global axes and per metre of bar, over the whole length of each bar:

qℓ​=ρARg(3.25)

For a bar with variable cross-section, the intensity follows ρA(x)g. The vector g is arbitrary: a constant horizontal acceleration is handled in the same way. The same load case applies their weight to the point masses at the nodes, to shells and to continuum elements; the self-weight of cables is handled by the cable analysis (chapter 7).

3.7.6 Thermal loads#

A thermal load consists of a uniform temperature change Δt and two gradients gy​=∂T/∂y and gz​=∂T/∂z, constant along the bar. It imposes the strain (2.14) of chapter 2. Its equivalent nodal forces, in local axes, are

f0=(−N∗, 0, 0, 0, My∗​, Mz∗​, N∗, 0, 0, 0, −My∗​, −Mz∗​)T,N∗=EAαΔt,My∗​=−EIy​αgz​,Mz∗​=EIz​αgy​(3.26)

Restrained at both ends, a uniformly heated bar carries a compressive force N=−EAαΔt; subjected to a gradient gz​, it carries the constant moment My​=−EIy​αgz​, which compresses the warm top fibre. Free on two supports, it deflects upwards by αgz​L2/8 at midspan. These results do not depend on Φ. A material whose coefficient of thermal expansion is zero cannot receive a thermal load: the calculation stops instead of returning a zero effect.

Note

A temperature profile that is nonlinear over the depth, such as those of EN 1991-1-5 §6.1.4.2, reduces to the two quantities of the bar through Δt=∫EbΔTdz/∫Ebdz and g=∫EbΔT(z−zˉ)dz/∫Eb(z−zˉ)2dz; the rest of the profile produces self-equilibrated stresses in the cross-section, which are not internal forces of the frame.

3.7.7 Imposed strains#

A generalised imposed strain (ε0​,κy​,κz​,κx​), with the convention (2.13), represents shrinkage, creep, prestress applied as a strain or a fabrication defect. It may be uniform over the bar, vary linearly from one end to the other, or follow a parabola defined by its values at the ends and at midlength. Its equivalent nodal forces are the work of the imposed strain in the strains of the shape functions,

f0=∫0L​BT(x)Ds​ε∗(x)dx,Ds​=diag(EA, GJ, EIy​, EIz​)(3.27)

where B gives (u′,θx′​,θy′​,θz′​) in terms of the twelve end displacements. Since the curvature functions of the Timoshenko beam are linear in x, these integrals are computed in closed form for the three variations. For a uniform field, the form of (3.26) is recovered with N∗=EAε0​, My∗​=EIy​κy​, Mz∗​=EIz​κz​ and a torsional moment T∗=GJκx​ (−T∗ at i, +T∗ at j). A statically determinate bar deforms freely under an imposed strain without any internal force; a statically indeterminate bar develops the forces that prevent this deformation.

3.7.8 Dislocations#

A dislocation is a discontinuity concentrated at abscissa a: axial gap Δu, jumps in deflection Δv, Δw, jump in twist Δθx​ and kinks κˉy​, κˉz​, integrals over a point of the imposed curvatures. The bending rotations therefore jump by Δθy​=−κˉy​ and Δθz​=−κˉz​. On the cantilever fixed at i, the dislocation moves end j, without any force, by

dj∗​=(Δu, Δv+Δθz​(L−a), Δw−Δθy​(L−a), Δθx​, Δθy​, Δθz​)T,f0=k⋅j​dj∗​(3.28)

where k⋅j​ denotes the six columns of the stiffness matrix associated with end j. The forces f0 are those that must be applied to bring the end back to its position; they account for shear through the matrix. A dislocation may be given in local or global axes. On a bar of length L fixed at both ends, a gap Δu produces the force N=−EAΔu/L; on a simply supported beam, a kink κˉy​ at midspan lowers that point by κˉy​L/4 without creating any internal force. A truss bar retains only the axial gap.

3.7.9 Surface loads distributed to the bars#

A uniform surface load q applied to a convex plane panel bounded by bars, with no slab in the model, is distributed to the edge bars by the usual geometric rules:

  • two-way distribution: each point of the panel goes to the nearest edge, which for a rectangle gives the triangles and trapezoids of the 45° lines;
  • one-way distribution: the panel is cut into strips parallel to a span direction d, and each strip carries half of its load to each of the two edges where it ends.

Edge k receives, at abscissa s, the line load

qk​(s)=qhk​(s)(two-way),qk​(s)=2qℓd​(s)∣d×tk​∣​(one-way)(3.29)

where hk​(s) is the depth of the region of edge k along its inward normal, ℓd​(s) the chord of the panel along d and tk​ the unit vector of the edge. These functions are piecewise linear: they become linear loads between their break points, and their sum is q times the area of the panel. On a 6 × 4 m panel, the long side receives a trapezoid of height 2q, and its moment at midspan, if simply supported, is 7.67q (in kN·m for q in kN/m²). A loaded edge segment with no bar is an error.

3.8 Bars on elastic foundation#

A bar may rest on a Winkler soil, with moduli kfx​, kfy​, kfz​ in translation and kmx​ in torsional rotation, per unit length. The element then carries a foundation matrix, consistent with the Hermite shape functions NH​ in bending and the linear ones in tension and torsion,

ksoil​=∫0L​NHT​diag(kfx​,kfy​,kfz​,kmx​)NH​dx(3.30)

added after the condensation of the releases (a hinge releases the moment of the bar, not the reaction of the soil) and never counted in the internal forces of the bar. This consistent foundation is an approximation that converges as the bar is subdivided; for a prismatic Euler-Bernoulli bar without release, linear statics replaces it with the exact beam-on-elastic-foundation element. Chapter 6 describes both and their fields of use.

3.9 Mass matrix#

Dynamic analyses (chapter 15) use the mass of the bars, computed from the density of the material. The consistent matrix combines a linear interpolation for axial and torsional motion with the cubic Hermite functions (without shear) for bending; in the xy plane, for (vi​,θzi​,vj​,θzj​),

mxy​=420ρAL​​15622L54−13L​22L4L213L−3L2​5413L156−22L​−13L−3L2−22L4L2​​+6ρIz​L​​0000​0201​0000​0102​​(3.31)

the second term being the rotary inertia of the cross-section, the rotation being interpolated linearly between the ends for it. The axial and torsional blocks are ρAL/6 and ρIp​L/6 times [21​12​], Ip​ being the polar moment of inertia (Iy​+Iz​ by default). The lumped matrix places ρAL/2 on each translation and ρIp​L/2 on each torsional rotation. A truss bar receives the consistent mass of linear interpolation on its three translations. Rigid arms transform the mass as they do the stiffness. This matrix is not exact: subdividing the bar is necessary in dynamics, as chapter 15 explains.

3.10 Scope and limits#

The keta beam element is exact, in the sense of beam theory, for the linear statics of a prismatic bar or a bar with variable cross-section under the loads described above. Its limits are as follows:

  • the bar is straight; a curved beam is represented by a sequence of bars;
  • the shear centre coincides with the centroid, and the axes of the cross-section are its principal axes: an asymmetric cross-section must be described in its principal axes, with the angle β orienting them;
  • torsion is uniform; restrained warping is covered in chapter 19;
  • the mass and the geometric stiffness are consistent approximations (chapters 15 and 17), and remain those of the constant cross-section on a bar with variable cross-section;
  • a bar of zero length, a beam bar with a zero second moment of area, an offset truss bar or a truss bar on elastic foundation, a bar with variable cross-section fitted with releases, a release that turns the bar into a mechanism, a load outside the bar, a thermal load on a material without a coefficient of thermal expansion and any non-finite input are refused by an error that names them.
