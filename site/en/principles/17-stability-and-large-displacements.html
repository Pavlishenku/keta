Chapter 17 Stability and large displacements

A member in compression loses bending stiffness: the beam-column deflects more than the linear analysis predicts, and beyond a certain load it no longer finds an equilibrium close to its initial shape. This chapter covers the three ways in which keta accounts for this effect. The first linearises the loss of stiffness about a field of axial forces: this is the geometric stiffness, used for linear buckling (critical load and modes) and for second-order analysis (the P-Δ effect). The second follows the structure through large displacements and large rotations, with a corotational formulation, for flexible structures, limit points and the geometric collapse load. The third keeps the tangent of an equilibrium state in order to compute on it natural modes or the buckling of an additional load. Lateral-torsional buckling of thin-walled members is treated here as far as the statement of the problem goes; the seven-degree-of-freedom element that computes it is described in chapter 19. Material nonlinearity (plastic hinges, fibres) is the subject of chapter 18; dynamics in large displacements, of chapter 16; catenary cables, of chapter 7.

17.1 Assumptions#

  • the material remains linearly elastic throughout this chapter; strains are small, even when the displacements and rotations are large;
  • for geometric stiffness and second order, the displacements remain small compared with the dimensions of the structure: equilibrium is written on the initial geometry, the axial force of each bar is constant along the bar, and only its work in the rotations of the bars is retained;
  • in the corotational formulation, the nodal rotations are arbitrary; each element remains straight in its own frame, where it keeps its linear stiffness;
  • loads are conservative unless stated otherwise: a force given in global axes keeps its direction; a bar load given in local axes follows the bar (follower load);
  • linear buckling assumes that the structure keeps its initial shape up to the critical load: it ignores imperfections and post-buckling behaviour;
  • warping of the cross-sections is not represented by the bar element with six degrees of freedom per node; torsion in it is Saint-Venant torsion (chapter 19 for non-uniform torsion).

17.2 Geometric stiffness#

A bar under the axial force N (positive in tension) and displaced transversely by v(x) receives, through the work of N in the rotation of its fibres, the additional energy

Ug​=2N​∫0L​(v′2+w′2+r02​θx′2​)dx,r02​=AIy​+Iz​​(17.1)

The third term is the Wagner term: an axial force that rotates with the section about its axis twists it, and compression reduces the torsional stiffness. With the cubic shape functions of bending, (17.1) gives the consistent geometric stiffness matrix. In the (x,y) plane, on the degrees of freedom (vi​,θz,i​,vj​,θz,j​),

Kg​=LN​​6/5L/10−6/5L/10​L/102L2/15−L/10−L2/30​−6/5−L/106/5−L/10​L/10−L2/30−L/102L2/15​​(17.2)

and the same matrix, up to the sign of the coupling terms, in the (x,z) plane. The Wagner term adds Nr02​/L on the torsional rotations of the two ends. A truss bar or a straight cable receives only the chord stiffness N/L on its transverse translations. The matrix (17.2) is linear in N: the tangent stiffness of a state of axial forces N is K+Kg​(N). Compression reduces it, tension increases it. Pinned ends condense Kg​ with the same operator as the elastic stiffness, which is the usual approximation. The shape functions are those of the Euler-Bernoulli beam, even when the elastic stiffness accounts for shear.

Shell facets receive their own geometric stiffness, built on the membrane forces (nx​,ny​,nxy​) (chapter 24). A rigid link with a lever arm receives the inverted-pendulum term: the force P that the slave transmits to the arm r adds on the rotations of the master the matrix (P⋅r)I−(PrT+rPT)/2. An elastic link between two distinct nodes has no second-order term modelled: buckling refuses it.

17.3 Linear buckling#

17.3.1 Eigenvalue problem#

Linear buckling seeks the multiplier λ by which a reference load case must be amplified for the tangent stiffness to become singular. The case is first solved linearly; its field of axial forces Nref​ (and its shell membrane forces) is frozen, and then

(K+λKg​(Nref​))φ=0(17.3)

is solved. The smallest positive values of λ are the critical factors, the φ the buckling modes. keta solves the problem in the inverted form Kg​x=μKx, which puts on the factorised side the matrix K, which is positive definite. The critical factor is λ=−1/μ: the smallest positive factors correspond to the most negative μ. Up to 600 free degrees of freedom, the problem is reduced by the Cholesky factorisation of K and solved densely; beyond that, by the Lanczos method in the inner product of K, with full reorthogonalisation. A pair is retained only if μ<−10−14 and if its relative residual ∥Kg​x−μKx∥/(∥Kg​x∥+∣μ∣∥Kx∥) is below 10−6. The modes are normalised to a unit peak: their largest component is +1.

A case that creates no axial force, or in which all the bars are in tension, gives no critical factor: the result is empty. The factors multiply the whole case, including the loads that compress nothing. To separate a permanent load from a load that increases, two-state buckling is used.

The matrix (17.2) is cubic: the compressed bars must be subdivided. For a pin-ended column, the error on the Euler load π2EI/L2 decreases as h4:

elements per bar124816
error on Pcr​+21.6 %+0.75 %+0.051 %+0.0033 %+0.0002 %

A single element gives 12EI/L2, that is 12/π2=1.216 times the exact load. The error is always on the high side: the consistent stiffness overestimates the critical load.

17.3.2 Two-state buckling#

On a structure, part of the axial force is fixed (self-weight, prestress) and the other part increases (imposed loads). Two-state buckling freezes two fields, that of a constant case Nc​ and that of a variable case Nv​, and seeks the multiplier of the variable part alone:

(K+Kg​(Nc​)+λKg​(Nv​))φ=0(17.4)

The problem is solved as (17.3), with K′=K+Kg​(Nc​) in place of K. This matrix must be positive definite: if the constant state already makes the structure buckle, the analysis stops, which keta checks on the inertia of the factorisation. By default, the bars in tension are excluded from both fields (N taken as min(N,0)): tension does not stiffen, which is on the safe side and prevents a highly tensioned part from stiffening the structure artificially. An option restores tension stiffening. A zero variable field is refused, unless a uniform unit compression in all the bars is explicitly requested. The constant case may be omitted: (17.3) is then recovered on the variable case, without tension stiffening. For a pin-ended column under a constant 0.4Pcr​ and a unit variable load, the multiplier is 0.6Pcr​.

17.3.3 Bisection on the inertia#

The eigenvalue computation may, in principle, miss a mode. keta gives an independent check through Sylvester's law of inertia (chapter 4): the number of negative pivots of the LDLT factorisation of K+λKg​ is the number of critical factors lying between 0 and λ,

n−​(K+λKg​)=#{i:0<λi​<λ}(17.5)

The first factor is bracketed by doubling λ from 1 until a negative pivot appears, then the interval is halved down to a relative width of 10−9. An exactly singular factorisation counts as a switch. This computation gives only the first factor, without a mode, but it cannot skip one. It exists for simple buckling and for two-state buckling.

Instead of the consistent geometric stiffness, the bisection may use the exact stiffness of the beam-column (stability functions, below) assembled at the force λN: a single element per bar then gives the exact critical load, to within the tolerance of the bisection. A bar pushed beyond the range of its trigonometric functions counts as switched.

17.3.4 Modal imperfection#

The nonlinear analysis of a perfect structure does not leave its initial shape before bifurcation. To study its actual behaviour, it is given an initial geometric imperfection in the shape of a buckling mode:

Xk′​=Xk​+sφktrans​(17.6)

where Xk​ is the position of node k, φktrans​ the translational part of the mode normalised to a unit peak and s the chosen amplitude (for example L/300 for the bow of a column). keta builds a new model on these coordinates; the rotations of the mode are ignored. An arbitrary shape, given node by node, is accepted in the same way. The amplitude of the imperfection is a matter for the engineer and for the rules of EN 1993-1-1 §5.3 or EN 1992-1-1 §5.2; keta does not set it.

17.4 Second-order analysis#

17.4.1 Equilibrium and iteration#

Second-order analysis writes the equilibrium of the structure taking into account the work of the axial force in the displacements, but still on the initial geometry:

(K+Kg​(N(u)))u=F(17.7)

The axial force itself depends on the displacements. keta solves (17.7) by fixed-point iteration: starting from the linear solution, the axial force of each bar (constant along the bar) is derived from it, K+Kg​ is assembled and factorised, the system is solved, and the process is repeated. The axial force is updated at each iteration, but the matrix Kg​(N) is in the factorisation: the amplification of the displacements is obtained from the first solution, and the following iterations only correct the variation of N. The computation stops when

∥uk+1​−uk​∥∞​≤10−12∥uk+1​∥∞​and∥F−(K+Kg​)uk+1​∥∞​≤10−10s(17.8)

where s is the largest of the norms of F, Ku and Kg​u; at most 30 iterations are allowed. In a structure where the axial force does not depend on the displacements (a column loaded at its top), two iterations are enough: the first computes, the second checks.

At each iteration, keta checks on the inertia of the factorisation that K+Kg​ remains positive definite. If a negative pivot appears, the load exceeds the critical load of the current state: the analysis stops with this message, instead of returning an unstable equilibrium solution. The reactions include the term Kg​u, that is, the additional moments of the axial force in the displacements. The results cannot be superposed: each combination must be computed as a case. Imposed support displacements are not accepted.

17.4.2 Amplification#

For a cantilever of length L compressed by P and pushed at its tip by H, the exact solution of EIw′′′′+Pw′′=0 gives the tip deflection

δ=PkH​(tankL−kL),k=P/EI​(17.9)

which tends to HL3/3EI as P tends to 0 and becomes infinite at the critical load Pcr​=π2EI/4L2. Its ratio to the linear deflection is close to the classical amplification 1/(1−P/Pcr​). keta's computation with one element per bar and the stability functions reproduces (17.9) to machine precision; with the consistent stiffness, the error is 5.6 % at P=0.9Pcr​ for one element, and 0.03 % for four.

17.4.3 Stability functions#

For a bar of stiffness EI under a constant axial force, the equation EIw′′′′−Nw′′=q has an exact solution. With u2=−NL2/EI (positive in compression), the rotational stiffness of an end and the carry-over factor are

s=2−2cosu−usinuu(sinu−ucosu)​,c=sinu−ucosuu−sinu​(17.10)

and the matrix of the bar in a plane of bending reads, on (vi​,θi​,vj​,θj​),

k(N)=LEI​​(2s(1+c)−u2)/L2s(1+c)/L⋅⋅​s(1+c)/Ls⋅⋅​−(2s(1+c)−u2)/L2−s(1+c)/L(2s(1+c)−u2)/L2⋅​s(1+c)/Lsc−s(1+c)/Ls​​(17.11)

which is symmetric. At zero force, s=4 and c=1/2: the linear beam is recovered. In tension, hyperbolic functions replace the circular functions. For ∣u∣<1, the closed forms lose their significant digits through cancellation (the denominator is about u4/12); keta then uses their series expansions in u2, to sixteen terms, whose truncation is below 10−20. A bar compressed beyond its own first singularity (u≥2π) is refused.

With this option, the matrix of each bar is (17.11) at the force of the current iteration; the fixed-end forces of the span loads are recomputed under this force, and the moments at the output sections are those of the exact deflected shape, M=M1​+Nw. One element per bar is enough. These functions neglect shear deformation. Torsion carries the Wagner term, as in the consistent stiffness:

kT​=LGJ+Nr02​​,r02​=AIy​+Iz​​(17.12)

linear in N, hence exact for uniform torsion without warping: the torsional buckling load NT​=GJ/r02​ of a member of low torsional stiffness is found along this path as with the consistent stiffness. Warping does not enter; for an open section where it matters, the warping element of chapter 19 is used.

17.5 Corotational formulation#

17.5.1 Chord frame#

When the rotations are no longer small, the geometric stiffness is no longer sufficient: the geometry itself changes. keta then describes each bar in a frame that accompanies it in its rigid-body motion. The axis e1​ of this frame is the current chord of the bar, between its displaced ends:

e1​=ℓxj​−xi​​,ℓ=∥xj​−xi​∥(17.13)

The other two axes follow the mean rotation of the two nodes about the chord. The local axis t0​ (the initial y axis of the section) is rotated by the rotation of each node, their mean p=(Ri​t0​+Rj​t0​)/2 is taken, and e3​=e1​×p/∥e1​×p∥ is built, then e2​=e3​×e1​. The frame E=[e1​,e2​,e3​] thus depends only on the positions and rotations of the nodes. The nodal rotations are total rotation vectors θ, with matrix R=exp(θ) given by the Rodrigues formula; they are updated by addition, and brought back into the principal range when their norm exceeds 0.9π, so that an end can make a full turn.

17.5.2 Local strains and energy#

In the chord frame, the bar undergoes only small strains. keta measures them by seven quantities: the elongation of the chord and the rotations of each end relative to the frame,

d=( ℓ+ℓ0​ℓ2−ℓ02​​,  θˉi​,  θˉj​ ),θˉk​=log(ETRk​E0​)(17.14)

where E0​ is the initial frame of the bar and log the map that returns the rotation vector of a rotation matrix. The elongation is written in a form that does not subtract two nearly equal lengths, which preserves its precision for a long, slightly elongated element. Another measure of the rotations, the sine form θˉ=axial(Rˉ−RˉT)/2, is available; the two agree to third order and converge to the same solution as the mesh is refined. The sine form is limited to local rotations of about 44° (∣sinθˉ∣≤0.7).

The strain energy of the bar is that of the linear element, written on these strains:

W(q)=21​d(q)TKˉd(q)(17.15)

where q gathers the twelve degrees of freedom of the bar and Kˉ is the 7×7 local matrix of the Timoshenko element (chapter 3), restricted to the elongation and the six rotations. Condensed releases and bars of variable inertia enter through their own local block, which keeps the rigid-body modes. The large displacements are carried entirely by the change of frame: (17.15) reduces to the linear energy for small displacements, and a rigid-body rotation of the bar, whatever it is, gives it no energy. Rigid offset arms rotate with their node: the flexible end is at x+R(θ)a, without linearisation of the rotation. A Winkler foundation is attached to the ground along the initial axis and reacts to the absolute displacement of the current axis.

17.5.3 Consistent tangent#

The internal forces of the bar are the gradient of its energy, and the tangent matrix its second derivative:

f(q)=∂q∂W​,KT​(q)=∂q∂qT∂2W​(17.16)

keta computes them by second-order forward automatic differentiation: the energy is programmed once, on a number type that carries the value, the gradient and the Hessian matrix. The derivatives obtained are the exact derivatives of (17.15), to within rounding, with no finite-difference step. The tangent is therefore the true consistent tangent, and Newton's method converges quadratically. Close to the rest configuration, the rotation functions switch to their series expansions, so that the derivatives remain exact at θ=0.

The corotational bar keeps a straight element in its frame: the curvature of a bent bar is represented by the rotations of its ends relative to the chord, and the accuracy depends on the subdivision. For a cantilever rolled into a quarter circle by an end moment, the error on the position of the tip is 2 % of the length with 4 elements and 0.1 % with 16: convergence is in h2.

17.5.4 Loads and solution#

The equilibrium of the structure reads

r(u)=λF(u)−fint​(u)=0(17.17)

Nodal forces are fixed. A nodal moment fixed in space is not conjugate to the rotation vector but to the infinitesimal rotation; its generalised force is J(θ)TM, where J is the Jacobian of the exponential map, and therefore depends on the state. Bar loads (distributed, linearly varying, point loads, self-weight) are transferred to the nodes at each evaluation, in the current frame of the bar: an intensity given in global axes keeps its direction (dead load), an intensity given in local axes follows the bar. The load stiffness ∂F/∂u of these loads is added to the tangent, by the same automatic differentiation; the tangent is then no longer symmetric and keta factorises it by a sparse LU decomposition. Projected loads and the other load types are refused.

Newton's method solves KT​Δu=r(u). The step is followed by a line search (Crisfield, Non-linear Finite Element Analysis of Solids and Structures, vol. 1, §9.3): the fraction s of the step is accepted as soon as

​ΔuTr(u+sΔu)​≤0.8​ΔuTr(u)​or∥r(u+sΔu)∥∞​<∥r(u)∥∞​(17.18)

otherwise s is halved, at most twenty times. The first criterion is the energy criterion, which the exact Newton step minimises; the norm of the residual alone rejected excellent steps on slender bars, whose first iteration carries a large axial residual of no consequence. The computation stops when ∥r∥∞​≤10−8max(∥λF∥∞​,∥fint​∥∞​), in at most 40 iterations per increment. It also stops when the residual has fallen below the rounding floor of the elements, 64ε∥K∥∣X∣, and the last correction is negligible: the strain vector is a difference of positions, whose rounding error is of the order of ε∣X∣. The load may be applied in several equal increments; each increment starts from the secant prediction 2uk​−uk−1​, and falls back to uk​ if Newton fails from this prediction.

The same analysis accepts rigid links, written without linearisation for finite rotations (us​=um​+(R(θm​)−I)r, θs​=θm​), elastic links, measured in the mean frame of their two nodes, skew supports, shell facets in a corotational formulation independent of the element (chapter 24) and cables as exact elastic catenaries (chapter 7). It refuses coupled 6×6 elastic supports, chains of rigid links, links that restrain only part of the rotations, nonlinear link laws and continuum elements. The end forces returned are those of the element in its chord frame; the reactions are the element forces minus the external loads at the supported degrees of freedom.

17.6 Arc length and limit points#

Near a limit point, the load can no longer increase: the tangent becomes singular and load control fails. The arc-length method adds the load factor λ to the unknowns, and fixes the length travelled at each step in the space of displacements. keta uses Crisfield's cylindrical form:

ΔuTΔu=Δℓ2(17.19)

where Δu is the displacement increment since the last equilibrium point. The prediction follows the tangent: δuF​=KT−1​F, δλ=±Δℓ/∥δuF​∥, the sign being the one that continues in the direction of the previous step (ΔuprevT​δuF​>0). Each correction solves δur​=KT−1​r and δuF​=KT−1​F, then seeks δλ such that Δu+δur​+δλδuF​ remains on the cylinder:

aδλ2+bδλ+c=0,a=δuFT​δuF​,b=2δuFT​t,c=tTt−Δℓ2,t=Δu+δur​(17.20)

Of the two roots, keta retains the one whose resulting increment makes the smallest angle with the previous increment, which prevents the analysis from retracing its steps. A negative discriminant, a correction that does not converge in 20 iterations or a factorisation that fails causes the radius to be halved, at most eight times. The stopping criterion is Newton's, with a scale that does not vanish when λ passes through zero on a snap-through branch (the scale includes the maximum force of the elements and the norm of the reference load). The radius adapts: it is multiplied by 5/n​, where n is the number of iterations of the step, bounded between 1/2 and 2 per step and between Δℓ0​/16 and 4Δℓ0​ overall. The analysis stops at the requested number of steps, or when ∣λ∣ or the largest displacement reaches a given limit.

At each converged point, keta factorises the tangent and records its inertia, the number n−​ of its negative pivots. A change of n−​ between two points indicates that a limit point or a bifurcation has been crossed between them. The curve obtained is a sequence of converged equilibrium points; the maximum of the load lies between two points, and it is bracketed by the collapse-load computation below. Arc length accepts only nodal forces: nodal moments and bar loads, which depend on the state, are refused, as are catenary cables.

For the plane truss of the figure (two bars of half-span a and rise h, of axial rigidity EA), the equilibrium of the apex node lowered by w gives in closed form

P(w)=2EAℓ0​ℓ0​−ℓ​ℓh−w​,ℓ=a2+(h−w)2​(17.21)

with the engineering strain (ℓ−ℓ0​)/ℓ0​ measured by the corotational bar. The arc-length points lie on this curve to within 10−13, on either side of the limit point, on the descending branch and up to the inverted position; the inertia of the tangent changes to n−​=1 between the two limit points.

17.7 Collapse load#

The geometric collapse load is the smallest multiplier λ of the case for which the structure no longer has a stable equilibrium reachable under increasing load. keta brackets it without following the curve: it tries a multiplier, starting from the last stable state found, and decides whether it is stable. The trial fails in four cases:

  • Newton does not converge (no neighbouring equilibrium);
  • Newton converges, but the tangent of the state obtained has a negative pivot (unstable equilibrium);
  • the tangent at the middle of the segment between the starting state and the state obtained has a negative pivot: Newton has jumped onto a distant stable branch, crossing the unstable region (snap-through);
  • the factorisation is singular.

λ is doubled from 1 as long as the trial succeeds, up to 106 at most, then the interval is halved:

λk​=2k as long as the trial succeeds,[λinf​,λsup​]←unstable half of [λinf​,λsup​] until λsup​−λinf​≤10−6λsup​(17.22)

Each trial starts from the last stable state, which follows the increasing loading. The result is the interval [λinf​,λsup​] and the cause of the last failure, one of the four above (no convergence, unstable equilibrium, snap-through between two stable equilibria, singular tangent), which the returned message names together with the value of λsup​. If no failure occurs up to λ=106, the analysis stops and says so. On the von Mises truss above, the interval contains the maximum of (17.21), with a relative width of 8⋅10−7, and the failure is a lack of convergence. The computation is made on the elastic material: it is a collapse by geometric instability, not a plastic collapse (chapter 18).

17.8 Lateral-torsional buckling#

A beam bent in its plane of greater inertia may buckle out of that plane by moving laterally and twisting: this is lateral-torsional buckling. The problem couples the lateral bending v and the torsional rotation θ; it involves the warping constant Iw​, which the ordinary bar element does not carry. For a doubly symmetric beam on fork supports (lateral displacement and twist restrained, warping and bending rotations free) under uniform moment, the critical moment is

Mcr​=Lπ​EIz​GJ​1+L2GJπ2EIw​​​(17.23)

keta provides this closed form and its generalisation to monosymmetric sections, with Wagner coefficient βx​:

Mcr​=L2π2EIz​​[2βx​​+4βx2​​+π2EIz​/L2π2EIw​/L2+GJ​​](17.24)

For βx​=0, (17.24) reduces to (17.23). The moment factor C1​ of a non-uniform diagram is to be applied by the user.

For an arbitrary moment diagram, loads applied above or below the shear centre, or a chain of bars, keta solves the eigenvalue problem (K+λKg​)φ=0 with the element with seven degrees of freedom per node of chapter 19. The matrix Kg​ is built there from the second-order energy of the thin-walled bar (Trahair), which contains the axial force, the bending moments My​ and Mz​ of the case and the height of the loads. The bending-torsion coupling is a discretisation, which converges in h2 to h4: for a 6 m IPE 300 under uniform moment, the difference from (17.23) is 4⋅10−6 with 8 elements and 2⋅10−7 with 16. The computation covers doubly symmetric sections only; a monosymmetric section (βx​=0) is refused.

17.9 Computations on an equilibrium state#

17.9.1 Saved tangent#

Several checks require the stiffness of a structure as it stands under a given load: the frequencies of a pylon under the weight of the deck, those of a network of tensioned cables, the buckling of a construction load on a structure already under load. For this, keta keeps the tangent of an equilibrium state u∗,

KT​=−∂u∂r​​u∗​(17.25)

together with the structure that was linearised, the displacements and the axial force of each bar of the state. Depending on the starting state, this tangent is:

  • for a linear static case: K+Kg​(N,nshells​), with the membrane forces of the shells;
  • for a state in large displacements: the consistent corotational tangent of the converged state, catenaries included;
  • for a cable state (chapter 7): K+Kg​(N) of the framework and the tangent stiffnesses of the catenaries at the displaced position of their anchorages; the geometric stiffness of the framework is included, otherwise a mast compressed by its stays would vibrate as if it were unloaded;
  • for the end of a construction stage (chapter 10): the active substructure of the stage, with its moduli at the date of the stage, and the geometric stiffness of the cumulative axial force; catenary cables active in the stage are refused.

The tangent is stored symmetrised; its relative asymmetry ∥K−KT∥max​/∥K∥max​ is recorded together with a fingerprint of the original model and the convergence flag. The computations that use it refuse a tangent from another model, a non-converged state and an asymmetry greater than 10−8: a follower load gives a non-symmetric load stiffness, which symmetric eigenvalue problems cannot handle. Links with lever arms and continuum elements are refused.

17.9.2 Natural modes on a state#

The natural modes of the loaded structure are the solutions of

(KT​−ω2M)ϕ=0(17.26)

where KT​ is the saved tangent and M the consistent (or lumped) mass of the reference geometry. Tension stiffens and raises the frequencies, compression lowers them. For a pin-ended column under the force P, the first mode keeps the shape of a sine half-wave and its frequency satisfies

ω02​ω2​=1−Pcr​P​(17.27)

which keta recovers to within 10−7 with the tangent of a static case (32 elements); the frequency vanishes at the critical load of the state. The tangent of a corotational state gives a close frequency, which converges to the same value as the mesh is refined.

17.9.3 Buckling on a state#

The buckling of an additional load on the structure in its frozen state reads

(KT​+λKg​(Nadd​))φ=0(17.28)

where Nadd​ is the axial force of the additional load, solved linearly on the elastic stiffness of the same structure (the substructure of the stage, where applicable). The multiplier applies to the additional load only. For the pin-ended column already compressed by P0​, it equals Pcr​−P0​. An additional load that creates neither axial nor membrane force is refused, as is a tangent that is not positive definite: the state has already buckled on its own. A structure that includes catenary cables is refused by this computation: they have no linear geometric stiffness for an additional load.

17.10 Scope#
