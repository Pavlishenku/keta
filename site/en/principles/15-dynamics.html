Chapter 15 Dynamics

This chapter deals with the vibrations of the structure about its equilibrium state: free vibrations (natural frequencies and mode shapes), the response to an action that varies in time (accelerogram, harmonic force from a machine or from pedestrians, vehicle crossing the deck), and the maximum response to an earthquake described by a spectrum. The problem set is the equation of motion of a linear structure, discretised into finite elements. Two operators are added to the stiffness of the static analysis: the mass, which produces the inertia forces, and the damping, which dissipates energy. Everything described here remains linear: the responses can be superposed, and the modes form a basis on which most of the calculations are decomposed.

Non-linear dynamics (large displacements, isolators and dampers, rupture of a member) is the subject of chapter 16; the dynamics of railway bridges under trains (HSLM model, dynamic factor) that of chapter 13; the response to turbulent wind that of chapter 22. Linear buckling, which shares the eigenvalue solver of this chapter, is treated in chapter 17.

15.1 Assumptions#

  • the displacements are small: the geometry, the mass and the stiffness are those of the reference configuration and do not vary during the motion;
  • the materials are linearly elastic; links with a non-linear law (gaps, unilateral supports, multilinear laws) are not admitted in these analyses, they belong to chapter 16;
  • dissipation is represented by an equivalent viscous damping: damping ratio per mode, or Rayleigh matrix αM+βK;
  • the stiffness is that of the linear static analysis (chapters 3 and 4), possibly increased by the geometric stiffness of a loaded state for the modes; catenary cables enter it only through their substitute model or through the tangent of a cable state (chapter 7);
  • the ground is rigid and all the supports move together under an earthquake (uniform excitation); the responses are relative to the supports.

15.2 Equation of motion#

The unknown is the vector u(t) of the displacements of the free degrees of freedom: three translations and three rotations per node, less those restrained by the supports and those the kernel restrains because no element gives them stiffness (chapter 4). Rigid links are eliminated by the same master-slave reduction as in statics. Dynamic equilibrium reads

Mu¨+Cu˙+Ku=F(t)(15.1)

where K is the assembled stiffness of the static analysis, M the mass, C the damping and F(t) the equivalent nodal forces of the loads at time t. Loads distributed along the bars are transferred to the nodes by the shape functions of the element, as in statics. The internal forces in the bars are then derived from the nodal displacements by the static recovery of chapter 4; the inertia forces distributed along a bar are not redistributed in this recovery, so that the internal force diagrams are accurate only to within the fineness of the mesh.

15.3 Mass matrix#

The mass of a bar is distributed along its length, whereas the finite element knows only the displacements of its two nodes. One must therefore choose how this mass is transferred to the nodal degrees of freedom. keta offers the consistent mass, built with the shape functions of the element, and the lumped mass, which places half of the mass at each end. The consistent mass is the default choice.

15.3.1 Consistent mass of bars#

The consistent mass is the kinetic energy of the bar written with the displacement field that the element interpolates between its nodes:

me​=∫0L​ρANT(x)N(x)dx+∫0L​ρNθT​(x)Iρ​Nθ​(x)dx(15.2)

The first term is the translational mass: linear functions for the axial displacement, cubic Hermite polynomials for the two deflections. In each bending plane, it gives the classical block

mflex​=420ρAL​​15622L54−13L​22L4L213L−3L2​5413L156−22L​−13L−3L2−22L4L2​​(15.3)

on the degrees of freedom (deflection, rotation) of the two ends, and ρAL/6[21;12] on the axial displacements. The second term is the rotary inertia of the cross-sections: about the axis of the bar, ρIp​ with the polar moment of inertia of the section (failing that, Iy​+Iz​); about the two principal axes, ρIy​ and ρIz​. These three terms are interpolated linearly in rotation and each gives a block ρIL/6[21;12]. The shape functions are those of the Euler-Bernoulli beam, even when the stiffness accounts for shear deformation. The rigid offset arms enter the mass as they enter the stiffness, through the product TTme​T of chapter 3.

With rotary inertia, the bar behaves as a Rayleigh beam. On simple supports, the frequency of mode n is then that of the Euler-Bernoulli beam divided by 1+(nπr/L)2​, where r=I/A​ is the radius of gyration. For a 20 m beam of rectangular section 2 m deep (r=0.577 m), divided into 40 elements, keta gives for the first three modes the ratios 0.995917, 0.98401 and 0.96522 to the Euler-Bernoulli frequency; the formula gives 0.995913, 0.98395 and 0.96492. The consistent mass slightly overestimates the frequencies on a coarse mesh (the Rayleigh quotient of a field that is too stiff): the bars must be subdivided to represent the higher modes, which statics did not require. On the same beam, the third bending frequency (three half-waves) is too high by 3.8 % with four elements, by 0.8 % with eight.

A truss member has no bending field: its consistent mass is that of the linear interpolation on the three translations, ρAL/6[21;12] in each direction, and its torsional inertia is lumped at the ends.

15.3.2 Lumped mass#

The lumped mass is diagonal. At each end of the bar, on (ux​,uy​,uz​,θx​,θy​,θz​) in local axes, it is

mnode​=2L​diag(ρA, ρA, ρA, ρIp​, 0, 0)(15.4)

with no mass on the bending rotations. It therefore neglects the rotary inertia of the sections: on the previous example, it converges to the Euler-Bernoulli frequency (ratio 0.99999997 with 40 elements), and not to that of the Rayleigh beam. It is used when the mass must remain diagonal, in particular in large displacements (chapter 16). The choice between the two forms is made for the whole model and may be changed bar by bar. A diagonal form supplemented by a rotary inertia ML2/78 about the local axis z (the lumping of Hinton, Rock and Zienkiewicz) is also available bar by bar.

Shells receive a consistent translational mass ρt per unit area and a rotary inertia ρt3/12, with no mass on the drilling rotation about the normal (chapter 24); continuum elements receive their consistent mass or, in the lumped variant, the same mass diagonalised with the total mass preserved (chapter 25). A catenary cable carries its mass wL0​/g, half at each end on the three translations.

15.3.3 Added masses#

Superstructures, equipment, fills or entrained water are not bars: they are represented by added masses, of four kinds.

  • a point mass m on a node, on its three translations, with rotary inertias about the global axes;
  • an arbitrary symmetric 6×6 nodal mass matrix, given by its diagonal or its lower triangle, which replaces the previous one or is added to it;
  • a mass per unit length on a bar, uniform, linear or parabolic along the bar (μ(x) in kg/m), possibly offset; it is integrated with the shape functions of the consistent mass by a five-point Gauss quadrature, exact for the parabolic law, or shared between the two nodes as ∫μ(1−ξ)dx and ∫μξdx; it is a translational mass, without rotary inertia of its own;
  • the suppression of the automatic mass ρA of a bar, when the mass is supplied otherwise.

A mass offset by e from the node that carries it follows the rigid-body kinematics um​=u+θ×e. Its nodal matrix is

Mnode​=TT[mI0​0J​]T=[mImS(e)​−mS(e)J+m(∣e∣2I−eeT)​],T=[I0​−S(e)I​](15.5)

where S(e) is the matrix of the cross product with e. The rotation block is the parallel axis theorem. This matrix is obtained either by giving it as a nodal matrix, or by placing the mass on a node connected to the structural node by a rigid link: the master-slave reduction produces the same matrix TTMT.

15.4 Eigenmodes#

15.4.1 Eigenvalue problem#

Without load or damping, equation (15.1) admits solutions u=φsinωt that satisfy

(K−ω2M)φ=0(15.6)

ω is the natural circular frequency (rad/s), f=ω/2π the frequency and φ the mode shape. K is symmetric positive (definite as soon as the structure is restrained), M symmetric positive but often singular: a lumped mass or point masses leave rotations without mass. So as never to factorise M, keta solves the inverted problem, with a spectral shift σ:

Mφ=μ(K−σM)φ,ω2=σ+μ1​(15.7)

The lowest frequencies correspond to the largest values of μ, those that iterative methods find first; the massless directions give μ=0 and are discarded. When K is positive definite, σ=0. When it is not (free body, mechanism stabilised by the mass alone), keta takes

σ=−10−8imax​Mii​Kii​​(15.8)

over the degrees of freedom that carry mass. The ratio Kii​/Mii​ is the Rayleigh quotient of a unit vector: it is of the same order as the highest eigenvalue. The factor 10−8 makes K−σM positive definite without degrading its conditioning beyond 108. The rigid-body modes then come out first, at zero frequency (∣ω2∣≤10−6∣σ∣). The matrix is judged positive definite when its LDLT factorisation has neither a negative nor a zero pivot, and the smallest pivot exceeds 10−13 times the largest.

15.4.2 Solution method#

A model of at most 600 free degrees of freedom is solved directly: Cholesky reduction and computation of all the eigenvalues. Beyond that, keta uses the shifted block Lanczos method, in the form of Grimes, Lewis and Simon: a Krylov space built in blocks of six vectors, orthonormalised for the inner product xT(K−σM)y with full reorthogonalisation, each block being obtained by a solution with the matrix K−σM factorised once. A block of six vectors captures in one pass a frequency of multiplicity up to six: the two bending modes of a pier of square section, the six rigid modes of a free body. A single-vector method contains only one direction per eigenspace and can miss the second mode of a pair without anything signalling it.

Every five steps, the error estimate of the Ritz values (the product of the last coefficient of the recurrence and the last component of the Ritz vector) is compared with 10−12∣μmax​∣; the iteration stops when the requested modes pass this test, at the latest after 8n+65 vectors for n requested modes. Each pair retained is then checked by its dimensionless residual:

r=∥Mφ∥+∣μ∣∥(K−σM)φ∥∥Mφ−μ(K−σM)φ∥​≤10−6(15.9)

A pair that does not satisfy this criterion is rejected, and a warning signals that the modes returned may not be the lowest.

15.4.3 Sturm count#

The Lanczos stopping test bounds the error of the modes found; it says nothing about a missing mode. keta checks this by Sylvester's law of inertia: the number of eigenvalues ω2 below a value c equals the number of negative pivots of the factorisation of K−cM,

#{ωi2​<c}=neg(LDLT of K−cM)(15.10)

The count is made at a cut-off c placed just above the highest mode retained (10−6 relative). If it announces more eigenvalues than modes found below it, the Lanczos method restarts on a fresh block, made orthogonal to the modes already found, up to ten times; a discrepancy that remains is reported, never passed over in silence. If c falls exactly on an eigenvalue (zero pivot), it is raised by 10−9 in relative terms. The same count gives the number of modes in a frequency band [f1​,f2​], as the difference of the counts at (2πf2​)2 and (2πf1​)2.

Three criteria set the number of modes computed:

  • a number of modes (ten by default);
  • a cut-off frequency: the Sturm count gives the number of modes below it, keta computes them all and checks that it has found them;
  • a fraction of the mass: the number of modes is increased in increments (ten by default) until, in each translational direction that carries mass, the sum of the effective masses reaches the requested fraction of the total mass, typically 90 % (EN 1998-1 §4.3.3.3.1).

A second method, inverse iteration with deflation (mode by mode, convergence of the Rayleigh quotient to 10−12 in at most 2000 iterations), is available as an independent check.

15.4.4 Normalisation, participation and effective masses#

The modes are mass-normalised, φiT​Mφi​=1, and oriented so that their largest component is positive; for display, each mode shape is also returned with its largest component equal to 1. For an overall ground motion in direction d, let rd​ be the influence vector: the unit rigid-body displacement in that direction, a translation, or a rotation about the origin of the global axes (then r equals ed​×x on the translations of a node at position x). The participation factor and the effective mass of mode i are

Γi,d​=φiT​(Mff​rf​+Mfs​rs​),mi,d​=Γi,d2​(15.11)

The indices f and s denote the free degrees of freedom and those of the supports. The term Mfs​rs​ is the consistent-mass coupling between the free nodes and the supports that move with the ground: without it, the seismic force of a consistent mass is wrong near the supports, by an error that decreases as the square of the element size (Chopra, Dynamics of Structures). keta takes it into account by default; it is zero for a lumped mass. The total mass set in motion is then

Mtot,d​=cTMff−1​c,c=Mff​rf​+Mfs​rs​(15.12)

and the sum of the effective masses of all the modes equals it: the ratio ∑i​mi,d​/Mtot,d​ measures the share of the mass represented by the computed modes. Without coupling, Mtot,d​=rfT​Mff​rf​. The mass carried directly by the support nodes is not part of the moving mass: for a 30 m, 75 t mast fixed at its base, divided into 150 elements, the total horizontal mass is 74.83 t.

On this mast of square section, keta finds the bending modes in pairs of equal frequencies (0.621634 Hz twice, then 3.890719 Hz twice); the Euler-Bernoulli frequency of a cantilever, 1.87512EI/ρA​/(2πH2), is 0.621765 Hz, the difference of 0.02 % being the effect of rotary inertia.

15.4.5 Modes about a loaded state#

A stay, a main cable or a highly compressed pier vibrate about a state of internal forces that modifies their stiffness. keta then computes the modes of the tangent stiffness

(K+Kg​(N)−ω2M)φ=0(15.13)

where Kg​(N) is the geometric stiffness of the axial forces of the state (chapter 17): tension stiffens, compression softens. The state is either a field of axial forces per bar, or a saved tangent: that of a static load case (shell membrane forces included), of a large-displacement equilibrium, of a catenary cable state (chapter 7) or of a construction stage (chapter 10). The mass remains that of the reference geometry. For a simply supported beam in tension N, the first mode satisfies

ω2=ω02​(1+π2EINL2​)(15.14)

keta recovers this ratio to within 4⋅10−9 (1.0463183 for NL2/π2EI=0.0463). A state beyond the critical load gives a negative eigenvalue ω2: the calculation stops and says so.

15.4.6 Rotating structures and substructures#

Two variants of the modal analysis serve particular cases. For a structure rotating at constant speed Ω about a fixed axis, keta solves, in the rotating frame,

(K+Kg​(Nc​)−Ω2M⊥​)φ=ω2Mφ(15.15)

where Nc​ is the axial force of the steady state under the centrifugal forces ρAΩ2P(x−p) (P projects onto the plane of rotation), integrated with its variation along each bar, and M⊥​=∫ρASTPSdx the softening due to the displacement of the mass in the centrifugal field. Gyroscopic Coriolis coupling is neglected.

For a large model, the Craig-Bampton modal synthesis condenses a group of bars and shells onto its boundary nodes:

uI​=ΨuB​+Φq,Ψ=−KII−1​KIB​(15.16)

where uI​ are the interior degrees of freedom, uB​ those of the boundary, Ψ the static constraint modes and Φ the first fixed-interface modes (none: Guyan condensation). The reduced problem is solved directly and its modes are expanded back onto the whole model. The frequencies so obtained are upper bounds (Rayleigh-Ritz method); they are those of the whole model when all the fixed-interface modes are kept.

15.5 Damping#

The damping of structures is not modelled from physical laws: it is represented by an equivalent critical damping ratio ξ, chosen from the codes (EN 1998-2, EN 1991-2 §6.4.6.3, footbridge guides) or from measurements. keta offers four ways of defining it.

15.5.1 Modal ratios#

A ratio ξ common to all the modes, or a ratio ξi​ per mode, is given. The damping is then assumed diagonal in the basis of the mass-normalised modes:

φiT​Cφj​=2ξi​ωi​δij​(15.17)

Each mode is an independent damped oscillator, and the matrix C is never built.

15.5.2 Rayleigh damping#

The matrix C=αM+βK is diagonal in the modal basis; it gives the mode of circular frequency ω the ratio

ξ(ω)=2ωα​+2βω​(15.18)

The coefficients are fitted to two modes of circular frequencies ω1​ and ω2​ and ratios ξ1​ and ξ2​:

α=ω22​−ω12​2ω1​ω2​(ξ1​ω2​−ξ2​ω1​)​,β=ω22​−ω12​2(ξ2​ω2​−ξ1​ω1​)​(15.19)

Between the two fitting frequencies, the ratio is below the target value; above, it grows linearly with ω, which damps the higher modes heavily.

15.5.3 Strain-energy damping#

A composite structure, or a deck on elastomeric bearings, combines materials with different damping. The ratio of each mode is then the mean of the ratios of the materials weighted by the strain energy that each stores in that mode (EN 1998-2):

ξi​=∑e​Ee,i​∑e​ξe​Ee,i​​,Ee,i​=21​φi,eT​ke​φi,e​(15.20)

The sum runs over the bars (catenary cables excepted), the shells and the elastic links, which have their own ratio; continuum elements are not admitted. For two identical ties in series, with ratios of 2 % and 6 %, keta returns 4 %.

15.5.4 High-frequency filter#

To extinguish the higher modes without affecting the useful modes, the modal ratios may be increased by a supplement dc​ beyond a cut-off frequency fc​:

ξi′​={ξi​ξi​+dc​​fi​≤fc​fi​>fc​​(15.21)

An explicit matrix exists only for Rayleigh damping; the other forms are ratios per mode. They can therefore be used only in the analyses that go through the modal basis:

15.6 Direct time integration#

15.6.1 Newmark scheme#

Equation (15.1) is integrated step by step, with a constant time step Δt. The Newmark scheme relates the displacements and velocities at the end of the step to the unknown acceleration u¨n+1​:

un+1​u˙n+1​​=un​+Δtu˙n​+Δt2[(21​−β)u¨n​+βu¨n+1​]=u˙n​+Δt[(1−γ)u¨n​+γu¨n+1​]​(15.22)

Substituting these relations into the equilibrium at the end of the step, the acceleration is the solution of the system

(M+γΔtC+βΔt2K)u¨n+1​=Fn+1​−Cv~−Ku~(15.23)

where u~ and v~ are the predictions of (15.22) without the term in u¨n+1​. Since the step is constant, the effective matrix is factorised once for the whole history. Two sets of parameters are offered:

  • average acceleration, γ=1/2, β=1/4 (trapezoidal rule), by default;
  • linear acceleration, γ=1/2, β=1/6.

The initial acceleration comes from equilibrium at t=0, Mu¨0​=F0​−Cu˙0​−Ku0​. When the mass is singular (massless rotations), this equation is solved with the effective matrix, which gives a zero acceleration on these degrees of freedom when starting from rest. The history starts from rest, or from a given initial state (u0​,u˙0​) (refused in the presence of rigid links). At each step, keta returns the displacements, velocities and accelerations of all the free degrees of freedom and the mechanical energy 21​u˙TMu˙+21​uTKu.

15.6.2 Accuracy and stability#

The behaviour of the scheme is read on an oscillator of circular frequency ω, with Ω=ωΔt. Without damping and with γ=1/2, the discrete solution is an oscillation of apparent circular frequency ωˉ=Ωˉ/Δt such that

cosΩˉ=1−2(1+βΩ2)Ω2​(15.24)

The amplitude is preserved: the scheme introduces no numerical damping, but lengthens the period. For average acceleration, (15.24) reads tan(Ωˉ/2)=Ω/2: the scheme is stable whatever the step, and the period elongation is about Ω2/12, that is 0.8 % at Δt=T/20 and 3.2 % at T/10. Linear acceleration is twice as accurate (Ω2/24) but is stable only if the right-hand side of (15.24) remains greater than −1, that is Ω≤23​ or

TΔt​≤π3​​≈0.551(15.25)

keta computes the discrete solution that these formulae describe. For an oscillator subjected to a step force F, the discrete solution is un​=(F/k)(1−cosnΩˉ): over 200 steps, the displacements computed by keta depart from it by less than 10−13F/k for both schemes and steps from T/20 to T/2. With linear acceleration, the response stays bounded at Δt=0.55T and diverges at 0.56T.

In practice, a step of at most T/10 to T/20 is needed for the shortest period that matters in the response. The modes of shorter period, which the mesh always contains, are not represented correctly; with average acceleration they stay bounded but are not damped.

15.6.3 HHT-α scheme#

The Hilber-Hughes-Taylor scheme damps these spurious modes. It keeps the relations (15.22) but writes the equilibrium at an intermediate instant of the step:

Mu¨n+1​+(1+α)(Cu˙n+1​+Kun+1​)−α(Cu˙n​+Kun​)=(1+α)Fn+1​−αFn​(15.26)

with α∈[−1/3,0], γ=(1−2α)/2 and β=(1−α)2/4. The scheme remains second order and unconditionally stable; its spectral radius tends, for modes whose period is small compared with the step, to (1+α)/(1−α): 0.82 for α=−0.1, 0.54 for α=−0.3, 0.5 for α=−1/3. On an oscillator integrated with the step Δt=104T, the sequence of displacements computed by keta is that of this scheme to within 10−7, and the spectral radius of its amplification matrix is 0.818, 0.538 and 0.500 for these three values. α=0 gives back average acceleration.

15.7 Modal superposition#

When the response is governed by a few modes, the equation of motion is projected onto the first n modes, u=∑i​φi​qi​(t). Since the modes are orthogonal with respect to M and K, and the damping is assumed modal, n independent oscillators are obtained:

q¨​i​+2ξi​ωi​q˙​i​+ωi2​qi​=φiT​F(t)(15.27)

Each is integrated by the same scheme (Newmark or HHT) reduced to a scalar equation, starting from rest (q¨​i​(0)=φiT​F(0)), then the displacements, velocities and accelerations are recombined. The ratio ξi​ is one of the forms of the previous section. The modes not retained contribute neither dynamically nor statically: there is no static correction for truncation. The number of modes is therefore set according to the effective mass mobilised and to the spatial distribution of the loads.

When the force is piecewise linear within the step, the oscillator (15.27) has an exact solution (Nigam and Jennings). For p(t)=p0​+st over the step,

q(t)=ω2p0​+st​−ω32ξs​+e−ξωt(C1​cosωD​t+C2​sinωD​t),ωD​=ω1−ξ2​(15.28)

where C1​ and C2​ come from the state at the start of the step. keta uses this solution where the accuracy must not depend on the step: response spectrum of an accelerogram, floor spectra and passage of trains (chapter 13). The step then serves only to sample the load.

15.8 Excitations#

15.8.1 Time-dependent loads#

The most general form of F(t) is a sum of static load cases each multiplied by a function of time, given by points and interpolated linearly:

F(t)=c∑​fc​(t)Fc​(15.29)

A periodic action of period T0​ is written as its Fourier series,

f(t)=a0​+k≥1∑​[ak​cosT0​2πkt​+bk​sinT0​2πkt​](15.30)

sampled at the instants tn​=nΔt. A force PsinΩt may also be applied directly to one component of a node.

15.8.2 Uniform support earthquake#

When all the supports undergo the same acceleration ag​(t) in direction d, the motion is written relative to the supports. The equivalent loading is

F(t)=−(Mff​rf​+Mfs​rs​)ag​(t)(15.31)

with the influence vector and the coupling to the supports of (15.11). The direction is a global translation or a rotation about a global axis through the origin. The displacements obtained are relative; the absolute acceleration of a point is obtained by adding ag​ in the direction of the excitation. Differential support motions (supports of distant piers excited out of step) are not treated.

15.8.3 Moving vehicles#

A vehicle is a set of axles (point loads at fixed distances from a reference point) and of segments of uniform load, which travels along a path of bars. Its position at time t is

r(t)=r0​+v0​t+21​at2(15.32)

(a=0: constant speed; a<0: braking, the vehicle stops at t∗=v0​/∣a∣ and remains still thereafter). At each step, each axle present on the path is applied as a point load on the bar that carries it and transferred to the nodes by the shape functions of the element; each segment is split according to the bars it covers and applied as a partial uniform load. An axle off the deck loads nothing. Only the vertical loads (in the direction of the vehicle) are applied: the longitudinal inertia force ma of a braking vehicle is not. With axles regularly spaced at D, resonance occurs at the speed v=Df1​; the speed sweep of trains, with the dynamic factor of EN 1991-2 §6.4.6, is described in chapter 13.

15.9 Harmonic and random response#

15.9.1 Steady harmonic state#

Under a force F(t)=Re(PeiΩt), where P is the vector of a load case, the steady-state response is computed by modal superposition, each mode having its complex transfer function:

u(Ω)=i∑​φi​ωi2​−Ω2+2iξi​ωi​ΩφiT​P​(15.33)

By default, all the modes of the model are superposed. Each mode must have a strictly positive damping ratio, otherwise the response at resonance would be infinite: keta refuses the calculation. Imposed settlements are not admitted. For each frequency of the sweep, keta returns the real and imaginary parts of the displacements, their amplitude ∣u∣=ur2​+ui2​​ and their phase. On request, the internal force diagrams are reconstructed in the same way: the real part by the static recovery with the span loads (in phase with the force), the imaginary part without them, the amplitude component by component. For an oscillator, keta gives back the amplification 1/(1−r2)2+(2ξr)2​ and the phase −arctan(2ξr/(1−r2)) to machine precision, for r=Ω/ω equal to 0.5, 1 and 1.5.

15.9.2 Random excitation#

When the amplitude of the loads of a case follows a stationary random process F(t)=α(t)P of spectral density Sα​(f) (loads fully correlated with one another), the spectral density of a response y and its standard deviation are

Sy​(f)=∣Hy​(f)∣2Sα​(f),σy​=∫Sy​(f)df​(15.34)

where Hy​ is the transfer function of (15.33). The integral is computed by the trapezoidal rule on the frequency grid supplied, which must therefore be fine around the resonances. The density Sα​ is one-sided and referred to the frequency in hertz. For an oscillator under white noise of density S0​, keta gives the standard deviation (P/k)S0​πf0​/4ξ​ to within 10−4 with 20 000 frequencies up to 5f0​. The standard deviations of the internal forces at the study sections are obtained in the same way.

15.10.1 Response spectra#

The earthquake is described by its pseudo-acceleration response spectrum Sa​(T,ξ): the maximum response of an oscillator of period T and ratio ξ. keta accepts three forms.

If it is given for a ratio ξtab​, it is corrected for the ratio of the mode by η(ξ)/η(ξtab​), possibly only from a given period onwards (the acceleration at zero period is often left uncorrected).

15.10.2 Response of each mode#

Mode i, of period Ti​ and ratio ξi​, responds at most, in direction d, by

ui​=Γi,d​ωi2​Sa​(Ti​,ξi​)​φi​,Vi​=Γi,d2​Sa​(Ti​,ξi​)=mi,d​Sa​(Ti​,ξi​)(15.37)

where Vi​ is the base shear of the mode. Rigid modes (zero frequency) do not contribute. The excitation may follow any unit translation vector v, for example a skew horizontal direction: since the participation is linear in the influence vector, Γi,v​=∑d​vd​Γi,d​.

Modes of the same frequency (to within 10−9), such as the two bending modes of a square pier, form an eigenspace whose basis is arbitrary: the calculation has fixed it at the whim of round-off. A quadratic combination rule is not invariant under rotation of this basis. keta therefore rotates the basis of each group of coincident modes, by an orthogonal transformation that preserves orthonormality, so that the first mode of the group alone carries the participation in the direction of the excitation. The result no longer depends on round-off, and it is that of the CQC, for which two coincident modes are fully correlated.

15.10.3 Combination of modes#

The modal maxima do not occur at the same instant. A response R (displacement, internal force, reaction) is combined from its signed modal values Ri​, by one of the following rules.

The square root of the sum of the squares (SRSS) assumes the modes independent:

R=i∑​Ri2​​(15.38)

The complete quadratic combination (CQC) accounts for the correlation of neighbouring modes:

  • the double sum of Rosenblueth and Elorduy, form (15.39) with ρij​=1/(1+εij2​), εij​=(ωi′​−ωj′​)/(ξi′​ωi​+ξj′​ωj​), ω′=ω1−ξ2​ and ξ′=ξ+2/(td​ω), where td​ is the duration of the strong phase of the earthquake (20 s by default);

On a cantilever of nearly square section excited at 45° (two modes at 1.510 and 1.584 Hz), the base shear is 410 kN by SRSS, 553 kN by CQC, 560 kN by double sum and 580 kN by the 10 % rule, which here adds the two modes in absolute value.

15.10.4 Missing mass#

The computed modes mobilise only part of the mass. The remaining mass responds as a rigid body, at the ground acceleration (the spectral acceleration at zero period). keta can add its contribution to the base shear:

Vmm​=(Mtot,d​−i∑​mi,d​)Sa​(0),V=Vmodal2​+Vmm2​​(15.40)

This correction applies only to the base shear, not to the displacements nor to the internal force diagrams: those are valid only for a modal basis that mobilises most of the mass.

15.10.5 Combination of directions#

The responses to the components of the earthquake in the three directions are combined component by component, either by SRSS or by the 30 % rule of EN 1998-1 §4.3.3.5.2:

R=gmax​(Rg​+0.3d=g∑​Rd​)(15.41)

where Rd​ is the combined (positive) response to direction d.

15.10.6 Internal forces, reactions and signs#

For each mode, the signed displacement field (15.37) is processed by the static recovery without load: it gives the internal forces at the study sections and the support reactions of the mode. Each quantity is then combined separately, section by section and component by component. The combined results are therefore positive: they are probable maximum values in absolute value, which are not concurrent with one another (the maximum moment and the maximum axial force of a section do not occur at the same instant). The sign and the physical shape are read on the modal responses, which are kept. Combined with the permanent loads, these results are used as symmetrical envelopes (chapter 5).

15.10.7 Spectrum of an accelerogram and floor spectra#

The pseudo-acceleration spectrum of an accelerogram is computed oscillator by oscillator with the step solution (15.28), exact for an accelerogram that is linear between its points:

For a very short period, it tends to the peak ground acceleration. The floor spectrum of a node is obtained in the same way: the absolute acceleration of the node, u¨abs​=∑i​φi​q¨​i​+ag​ (the second term only if the node is observed in the direction of the excitation), is computed by modal superposition with the exact steps, then its spectrum is computed for the damping ratio of the equipment.

15.11 Footbridges under pedestrian loading#

keta follows the method of the Sétra/AFGC guide Passerelles piétonnes : évaluation du comportement vibratoire sous l'action des piétons (2006). The guide classifies the footbridge by its traffic (classes I to IV), places each natural frequency in a range of resonance risk, derives from it the load cases to be studied, applies to the deck a harmonic load at the frequency of the mode, and compares the acceleration obtained with the comfort ranges. The kernel returns each of these quantities as a number; it does not pronounce a verdict.

15.11.2 Equivalent crowd load#

The N=dS pedestrians of a crowd of density d on the loaded area S walk neither at the same frequency nor in phase. The guide replaces them by neq​ pedestrians in phase, at the natural frequency:

neq​=10.8ξN​  (case 1, and case 3 in class II),neq​=1.85N​  (case 2, and case 3 in class I)(15.43)

with F0​ = 280, 140 and 35 N (vertical, longitudinal, lateral) for the first harmonic of walking, 70, 35 and 7 N for the second, which is used in case 3. The factor ψ(f) reduces the load as the frequency moves away from the walking frequency. For a class II footbridge, S = 300 m², ξ = 1 % and f = 1.9 Hz, keta gives neq​ = 16.73 and an amplitude of 15.62 N/m², equal to the formula.

15.11.3 Load following the mode#

The load of the guide is applied with the sign of the mode shape, so that all the contributions are adverse and the modal force is maximum. keta builds this load case from the displacement field of the element along each bar (linear in axial, cubic Hermite in bending), in the chosen global direction:

q(x)=qsgnφ(x)⟹φTF=q∫∣φ(x)∣dx(15.45)

A bar that contains a vibration node is cut at the zero of the cubic, found by bisection on each monotonic piece. As a variant, the load may be applied only where φ>0, like a load of constant direction. The resulting case is computed in the harmonic regime at the frequency of the mode (steady-state response at resonance), or in time with the function cos2πft. The maximum acceleration is then compared with the comfort ranges: vertical 0.5, 1 and 2.5 m/s², horizontal 0.15, 0.3 and 0.8 m/s², and, laterally, with the threshold of 0.10 m/s² beyond which pedestrians may synchronise with the movement of the deck.

15.12 Results of a time history and envelopes#

A time-history analysis produces, at each step, a state of the whole structure. The internal forces of a step are obtained by the static recovery without load of the displacement field of that step: they are the elastic forces Ku(t), read at the study sections, which balance the inertia and damping forces at the nodes. Reactions are returned only on the degrees of freedom actually restrained by a support: on a free degree of freedom of a support node, the nodal force is an inertia force, not a reaction. The envelope of a time history is formed, component by component, of the extremes over all the steps:

R+=nmax​R(tn​),R−=nmin​R(tn​)(15.46)

Each step remains available as a concurrent state: an envelope of chapter 5 that takes a time history as its source finds there, with the extreme value of a quantity, the other quantities of the same instant.
